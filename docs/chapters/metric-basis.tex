\chapter{Basis of the metric Vandermonde matrix}
\label{ch:metric-basis}

% Line colours of the plots in this chapter (monomial, Legendre, Chebyshev,
% Lagrange, ...)
\pgfplotscreateplotcyclelist{metricbasis}{%
  red\\blue\\black\\green!50!black\\orange\\violet\\}

The metric (trial) shape functions are computed from a generalized Vandermonde
matrix, i.e.\ from the values of a set of basis polynomials at the element nodes
(\cref{ch:theory}). Any basis of the polynomial space can be used for this
purpose. This chapter compares four bases, explains why the Lagrange basis of the
reference element is the default, and documents a limitation that no choice of
basis can remove, together with the hybrid trial space (\code{method=2}) that
avoids it. The numbers are produced by
\code{studies/metric_basis/study_metric_basis.m},
\code{probe_hybrid_space.m} and \code{study_hybrid_space.m};
the tests are in \code{tests/unit/MetricBasisTest.m} and
\code{tests/unit/MetricHybridTest.m}. The data in
\code{docs/figures/data/metric-*.dat} were computed with \texttt{MATLAB} R2026a
(IEEE double precision); values at round-off level can differ in the last digits
on other platforms and releases.

\section{Setting and basis invariance}

An element of degree $(p_\upxi,p_\upeta)$ has $n=(p_\upxi+1)(p_\upeta+1)$ nodes
$\vect x_k$ with skew coordinates $(s_k,t_k)$. Its metric shape functions $M_l$,
$l=1,\dots,n$, are polynomials of the space $Q_p(s,t)$ (degree $p_\upxi$ in $s$
and $p_\upeta$ in $t$, \cref{ch:theory}) and are interpolatory,
$M_l(\vect x_k)=\delta_{lk}$. To compute them, the code takes $n$ basis
polynomials $\phi_c$ of $Q_p(s,t)$, evaluates them at the nodes,
$V_{ck}=\phi_c(\vect x_k)$, and at the query points (Gauss or plot points),
$P_{cq}=\phi_c(\vect x_q)$, and solves
\[
  \matr V\,\matr N=\matr P,\qquad N_{lq}=M_l(\vect x_q).
\]

\paragraph{The basis does not change the shape functions.} If $\matr V$ is
non-singular, $Q_p(s,t)$ contains exactly one set of functions with the
Kronecker property $M_l(\vect x_k)=\delta_{lk}$, so every basis leads to the same
$M_l$. (A change of basis multiplies $\matr V$ and $\matr P$ from the left with
the same non-singular matrix, which cancels in $\matr V^{-1}\matr P$.)
Completeness, frame invariance and distortion immunity (\cref{ch:theory}) are
therefore not affected by the basis, and neither is unisolvence: whether
$\matr V$ is singular depends only on the node set and the space.

\paragraph{The basis changes the round-off error.} The system is solved by an LU
decomposition with partial pivoting. The relative error of the computed shape
functions is then bounded, up to a modest factor that depends on $n$, by
$\kappa(\matr V)\,\epsilon$, where $\epsilon\approx1.1\times10^{-16}$ is the unit
round-off of double precision \parencite{Higham2002Accuracy}. Roughly speaking,
every decade of the condition number $\kappa(\matr V)$ costs one significant
digit, so $\kappa(\matr V)$ is the quantity a good basis has to keep small.

\section{Candidate bases}

\code{PolynomialBasis1D(s, n, basis, ndist)} provides, with values and first
derivatives,
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lL@{}}
    \toprule
    \code{basis} & $\phi_a(s)$, $a=0,\dots,n$ \\
    \midrule
    \code{'monomial'} & $s^a$ (used without scaling) \\
    \code{'legendre'} & Legendre polynomials $P_a(s)$, three-term recurrence \\
    \code{'chebyshev'} & Chebyshev polynomials $T_a(s)$, three-term recurrence \\
    \code{'lagrange'} & Lagrange polynomials $\ell_a(s)$ through the 1D reference
      nodes \code{nodal_distribution(n, ndist)}, product formula (no division by
      $s-s_m$, so query points may coincide with nodes) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{center}
The 2D basis is the tensor product $\phi_a(\tilde s)\,\phi_b(\tilde t)$. The
Legendre, Chebyshev and Lagrange bases are defined on $[-1,1]$, but on distorted
elements the skew coordinates of the nodes leave $[-1,1]^2$ (up to $|s|=1.27$ for
the trapezoid of \cref{fig:test-elements}). Outside $[-1,1]$ these polynomials
grow quickly, e.g.\ $|T_p(s)|=\cosh(p\,\operatorname{arcosh}(|s|))$ for $|s|>1$,
which gives $T_{16}(1.27)\approx5\times10^4$. For these three bases
\code{ShapeFunctions2D_Metric} therefore maps the bounding box of the nodes in
$(s,t)$ onto $[-1,1]^2$,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde s = (s-c_\mathrm{s})/r_\mathrm{s},\qquad
  \tilde t = (t-c_\mathrm{t})/r_\mathrm{t},
\end{equation}
where $(c_\mathrm{s},c_\mathrm{t})$ is the centre and $(r_\mathrm{s},r_\mathrm{t})$
are the half widths of the box. The map is affine and computed from
frame-invariant quantities, so the space $Q_p$ and the frame invariance are
preserved; on a parallelogram it is the identity. The monomial basis is used
without this scaling.

\section{Analysis}

\paragraph{Affine elements.} On a parallelogram the skew coordinates of the nodes
are their reference coordinates, which form a tensor grid. $\matr V$ is then,
up to the order of its columns, the Kronecker product
$\matr V_\mathrm{t}\otimes\matr V_\mathrm{s}$ of the two 1D Vandermonde matrices,
and $\kappa_2(\matr V)=\kappa_2(\matr V_\mathrm{s})\,\kappa_2(\matr V_\mathrm{t})$:
for equal degrees the 2D condition number is the square of the 1D one. For
monomials on nodes that are symmetric about the origin, the 1D condition number
grows at least exponentially with $p$ \parencite{Gautschi1988Lower}. The
Lagrange basis on the same reference nodes is $1$ at one node and $0$ at all
others, so $\matr V$ is a permutation matrix and $\kappa(\matr V)=1$. The solve
then does not amplify round-off at all: metric and parametric functions agree to
round-off, and $\matr K_e$ is symmetric to round-off for every $p$.

\paragraph{Distorted elements, Lagrange basis.} The Lebesgue function of an
interpolation is the sum of the absolute values of all its shape functions at a
point; its maximum over the element, the Lebesgue constant, is the factor by
which the interpolation can amplify errors in the nodal values. Let
$\lambda_\mathrm{M}(\vect s)=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{n}|M_l(\vect s)|$ and
$\lambda_\mathrm{ref}(\vect s)=\sum\limits_{c=1}^{n}|\phi_c(\vect s)|$ be the
Lebesgue functions of the metric and of the reference interpolation, and let
$\hat{\vect s}_d$ be the reference nodes in the scaled skew coordinates (the
points where the Lagrange basis function $\phi_d$ is $1$). Then
\begin{equation}
  (\matr V^{-1})_{ld}=M_l(\hat{\vect s}_d),\qquad
  \kappa_1(\matr V)=\max_k\lambda_\mathrm{ref}(\tilde{\vect s}_k)\;
                    \max_d\lambda_\mathrm{M}(\hat{\vect s}_d).
  \label{eq:kappa-lagrange}
\end{equation}
The first relation holds because the matrix $\matr P$ of the points
$\hat{\vect s}_d$ is the identity, so $\matr N=\matr V^{-1}$ there; the second
follows from it, because the 1-norm of a matrix is its largest column sum.

With the bounding-box scaling all nodes $\tilde{\vect s}_k$ lie in $[-1,1]^2$,
so the first factor is bounded by the Lebesgue constant of the reference tensor
grid ($\le6.1$ for Gauss-Lobatto-Legendre (GLL) nodes and $p\le16$). Hence
$\kappa_1(\matr V)$ is essentially the Lebesgue function of the metric
interpolation at the reference nodes. The Lebesgue constant
$\Lambda_\mathrm{M}=\max_{\vect x\in\Omega_e}\lambda_\mathrm{M}(\vect x)$ is a
property of the space and the node set, not of the basis: it is the factor by
which the metric interpolation amplifies perturbations of nodal values (e.g.\ the
round-off of a computed displacement field), and it enters the interpolation
error,
$\|u-\mathcal I_\mathrm{M}u\|_\infty\le(1+\Lambda_\mathrm{M})
\inf_{q\in Q_p}\|u-q\|_\infty$, i.e.\ the interpolation error is at most
$1+\Lambda_\mathrm{M}$ times the best approximation error in $Q_p$.
\Cref{eq:kappa-lagrange} therefore states that the Lagrange basis adds no
ill-conditioning beyond that of the interpolation problem itself (up to the
factor $\lambda_\mathrm{ref}$ and the difference between $\lambda_\mathrm{M}$ at
the reference nodes and on the element). The orthogonal bases have no such
relation but behave similarly in practice (\cref{sec:metric-basis-results}); for
them $\kappa(\matr V)>1$ also on affine elements.

\section{Numerical results}
\label{sec:metric-basis-results}

\paragraph{Test cases.} Four of the single test elements of
\cref{fig:test-elements} (\code{usemTestElement}): parallelogram, trapezoid with
straight edges, 8-node element with two curved edges (\enquote{curved}), and
angular and curved-edge distortion combined (\enquote{general}); GLL nodes,
$p=1$--$16$. Measured quantities (\code{study_metric_basis.m}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\kappa_2(\matr V)$;
  \item $e_\updelta=\max_{l,k}|M_l(\vect x_k)-\delta_{lk}|$, the error of the
    computed shape functions for worst-case (unit) nodal data;
  \item $e_\mathrm{P}$, $e_{\nabla\mathrm{P}}$: errors of the reproduction of all
    monomials of total degree $\le p$ in normalized Cartesian coordinates and of
    their scaled gradients at the $(2p)^2$ Gauss points of \code{Ke_2D};
  \item $\Lambda_\mathrm{M}$, $\Lambda_\mathrm{N}$: Lebesgue constants of the
    metric and of the parametric interpolation, evaluated on a $(4p+9)^2$ grid in
    the element;
  \item $\|\matr K_e-\matr K_e^{\T}\|_\mathrm{F}/\|\matr K_e\|_\mathrm{F}$ on the
    parallelogram;
  \item at mesh level ($3\times2$ mesh of \cref{fig:test-meshes}, angular and
    curved-edge distortion $0.3$, \code{tests/integration/PatchTest.m}): the
    harmonic patch test of degree $p$ and the field
    $\vect u=(e^x\cos(y),\,-e^x\sin(y))$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Conditioning (\cref{fig:metric-basis-kappa}).} With monomials
$\kappa_2(\matr V)$ grows exponentially already on the parallelogram, from
$2.9\times10^2$ ($p=4$) over $2.9\times10^5$ ($p=8$) to $3.5\times10^{11}$
($p=16$). This is a factor of about $5.7$ per degree, close to
$(1+\sqrt2)^2\approx5.8$, i.e.\ about $(1+\sqrt2)^{p}$ for each of the two 1D
factors. On the parallelogram the Lagrange basis gives $\kappa_2=1$ for every
$p$, the Chebyshev basis $\approx2.6$, and the Legendre basis grows linearly
($55$ at $p=16$). On the distorted elements the three alternatives are within a
factor of $3$ of one another; the best of them improves on the monomials by a
factor $6$--$13$ at $p=4$ and $1$--$5\times10^4$ at $p=16$ (curved:
$1.4\times10^{13}$ for monomials, $3.1$--$4.8\times10^{8}$ for the alternatives;
general: $9.0\times10^{14}$ and $0.9$--$2.0\times10^{11}$). On the trapezoid the
monomial matrix at $p=16$ is rejected by the unisolvence check of
\code{ShapeFunctions2D_Metric} ($\operatorname{rcond}(\matr V)=1.9\times10^{-17}$,
below machine epsilon); at $p=15$ it gives $3.5\times10^{14}$, the alternatives
$0.5$--$1.1\times10^{11}$. Without the bounding-box scaling the alternatives
would lose more than three orders of magnitude on the trapezoid
($\kappa_2=2.2\times10^{15}$ instead of $8.6\times10^{11}$ for the Lagrange basis
at $p=16$).

\paragraph{Accuracy of the shape functions (\cref{fig:metric-basis-kron}).}
$e_\updelta$ follows $\kappa_2(\matr V)\,\epsilon$, as expected from the error
bound above. On the parallelogram the monomials give
$e_\updelta=2.0\times10^{-12}$ ($p=8$), $2.0\times10^{-9}$ ($p=12$) and
$1.2\times10^{-6}$ ($p=16$); with the Lagrange basis $e_\updelta\le10^{-28}$. On
the distorted elements at $p=16$ the monomials give $4.6\times10^{-5}$ (curved)
and $6.7\times10^{-4}$ (general) and are rejected on the trapezoid
($1.8\times10^{-3}$ at $p=15$); the Lagrange basis gives $6.8\times10^{-6}$
(trapezoid), $5.3\times10^{-9}$ (curved) and $1.8\times10^{-6}$ (general), and
the Legendre basis is equally good. For $p\le6$ all bases give
$e_\updelta<5\times10^{-13}$.

\paragraph{Polynomial reproduction (\cref{fig:metric-basis-leb}, right).} The
reproduction errors $e_\mathrm{P}$ and $e_{\nabla\mathrm{P}}$ are practically the
same for all bases, including the monomials, and follow
$\epsilon\,\Lambda_\mathrm{M}$ (trapezoid, $p=16$, Legendre, Chebyshev and
Lagrange basis: $e_\mathrm{P}=2$--$5\times10^{-9}$,
$e_{\nabla\mathrm{P}}=0.8$--$8\times10^{-8}$; at $p=15$ all four bases give
$e_\mathrm{P}=2$--$4\times10^{-10}$). The reason is the following. A polynomial
$g=\sum\limits_{c=1}^{n}c_c\,\phi_c$ of the space has the nodal values
$\vect f=\matr V^{\T}\vect c$. Its interpolant, computed with the LU factors of
$\matr V$, differs from $g$ by about $\vect c^{\T}\delta\matr V\,\matr N$, where
$\delta\matr V$ is the backward error of the LU decomposition; this is bounded by
$\epsilon\,\|\vect c\|\,\|\matr V\|\,\Lambda_\mathrm{M}$. For low-degree
polynomials the coefficients $\vect c$ and $\|\vect c\|\,\|\matr V\|$ are moderate
in every basis, so the error is of order $\epsilon\,\Lambda_\mathrm{M}$, whatever
$\kappa(\matr V)$ is. For generic nodal data (unit vectors as in $e_\updelta$,
round-off-contaminated or oscillatory fields), $\|\vect c\|$ can be as large as
$\|\matr V^{-1}\|\,\|\vect f\|$, and the error becomes of order
$\kappa(\matr V)\,\epsilon$. The element matrices are built from the individual
functions $M_l$ and $\nabla M_l$, not from reproduced polynomials. This is why the
asymmetry of $\matr K_e$ on the parallelogram follows $e_\updelta$
($\|\matr K_e-\matr K_e^{\T}\|_\mathrm{F}/\|\matr K_e\|_\mathrm{F}$, $0$ in exact
arithmetic):

(\code{docs/figures/data/metric-basis-mesh.dat}). Both quantities are of the
reproduction type: the exact solutions are polynomials or smooth fields with
rapidly decaying coefficients, so, as explained above, they are insensitive to
$\kappa(\matr V)$. For static results with $p\le12$ the choice of basis is
therefore irrelevant. The advantage of the Lagrange basis lies in the accuracy of
the individual shape functions and element matrices (symmetry on affine elements,
the Kronecker property used by the GLL nodal-quadrature mass matrix, generic
nodal data such as eigenvectors or wave fields), and it becomes decisive for
$p\gtrsim12$ ($e_\updelta$ of the monomials $\ge2\times10^{-9}$ on every test
element, up to $1.8\times10^{-3}$ at $p=15$; at $p=16$ the trapezoid is
rejected).

\paragraph{Cost.} All bases are evaluated with vectorized recurrences or
products; the Lagrange basis costs $\mathcal O(p^2)$ instead of $\mathcal O(p)$
operations per 1D function and point. Measured in \texttt{MATLAB} R2026a (one
computational thread), a call of \code{ShapeFunctions2D_Metric} with the $(2p)^2$
Gauss points takes $0.7$--$2.6$ times as long as with monomials for $p\ge4$ (no
systematic trend with $p$). In either case the solve with the
$(p+1)^2\times(p+1)^2$ matrix and the evaluation of the parametric functions
dominate the element routines.

\section{Choice and implementation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Default: Lagrange basis of the reference element in
    bounding-box scaled skew coordinates} (\code{p.metricBasis='lagrange'}). It
    is the only candidate that is exact on affine elements ($\matr V$ is a
    permutation matrix, so metric and parametric functions coincide to
    round-off and $\matr K_e$ is symmetric to round-off for all $p$); on
    distorted elements its condition number is given by the Lebesgue function of
    the metric interpolation (\cref{eq:kappa-lagrange}), and it is as accurate
    as the orthogonal bases. It uses the node distribution \code{p.ndist} of the
    element, so that $\matr V$ is a permutation matrix for every distribution.
  \item \code{'legendre'} and \code{'chebyshev'} are available for comparison;
    they are marginally better conditioned on the distorted test elements (by
    at most a factor of $3$), but not exact on affine ones.
  \item \code{'monomial'} uses $s^at^b$ without scaling. It is adequate for
    $p\lesssim6$ ($e_\updelta<5\times10^{-13}$), but ill-conditioned for large
    $p$ and should not be used for $p\gtrsim10$.
  \item \code{method=0} (dimensional global coordinates,
    \cite{Rajendran2003novel}) always uses monomials; the reference interval has
    no meaning there.
  \item With the default basis the full test suite (patch tests, Lee-Bathe
    benchmarks, element matrices, time integrators) passes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitation: The Lebesgue constant of
\texorpdfstring{$Q_p(s,t)$}{Qp(s,t)}}
\label{sec:metric-basis-outlook}

\Cref{fig:metric-basis-leb} shows that the Lebesgue constant
$\Lambda_\mathrm{M}$ of the metric interpolation grows exponentially with $p$ on
distorted elements, while that of the parametric interpolation,
$\Lambda_\mathrm{N}$, grows only logarithmically ($\Lambda_\mathrm{N}\approx6.0$
at $p=16$). This is a property of the space $Q_p(s,t)$ on the mapped node set;
since the shape functions do not depend on the basis, no basis can remove it.
Its consequences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Round-off floor.} Perturbations of nodal values are amplified by
    up to $\Lambda_\mathrm{M}$, and by \cref{eq:kappa-lagrange} the shape
    functions carry errors of order $\epsilon\,\Lambda_\mathrm{M}$ even with the
    best basis. On the trapezoid of \cref{fig:test-elements} ($|s|\le1.27$),
    $\Lambda_\mathrm{M}\approx8\times10^2$ at $p=8$ and $4\times10^7$ at $p=16$.
  \item \textbf{Approximation.} For fields outside the trial space the bound of
    the interpolation error contains the factor $1+\Lambda_\mathrm{M}$. Whether
    the Petrov-Galerkin solution inherits this depends on the discrete inf-sup
    constant, which is not analysed here (see \cref{sec:metric-hybrid} for
    mesh-level results).
\end{itemize}
The growth is not a consequence of completeness in $x$ and $y$: completeness
only requires the polynomials of total degree $\le\min(p_\upxi,p_\upeta)$ in $x$
and $y$, and the remaining functions of the trial space can be chosen
differently. This leads to the hybrid trial space of the next section.

\section{Hybrid trial space (\texttt{method=2})}
\label{sec:metric-hybrid}

\paragraph{Definition.} With $m=\min(p_\upxi,p_\upeta)$ and the Legendre
polynomials $L_a$, the hybrid trial space is
\begin{equation}
  T_p = P_m(\tilde s,\tilde t)\;\oplus\;\operatorname{span}\{L_a(\xi)L_b(\eta):\
  a\le p_\upxi,\ b\le p_\upeta,\ a+b>m\}.
  \label{eq:hybrid-space}
\end{equation}
The first part, $P_m(\tilde s,\tilde t)$, consists of all polynomials of total
degree $\le m$ in the bounding-box scaled skew coordinates $(\tilde s,\tilde t)$;
the code spans it with the products $L_a(\tilde s)L_b(\tilde t)$, $a+b\le m$.
Because $\tilde s$ and $\tilde t$ are affine (linear plus constant) functions of
$x$ and $y$, and vice versa, a polynomial of total degree $m$ in
$(\tilde s,\tilde t)$ is a polynomial of total degree $m$ in $(x,y)$ and vice
versa: $P_m(\tilde s,\tilde t)=P_m(x,y)$. The hybrid space therefore contains all
complete polynomials of degree $m$ in $x$ and $y$ (completeness, hence distortion
immunity and exact patch tests), and, since $(\tilde s,\tilde t)$ move and rotate
with the element, it is frame invariant. The second part, the complement, is
written in the parametric coordinates $(\xi,\eta)$, in which the element nodes
form a tensor grid.

A metric trial space must have the dimension $(p_\upxi+1)(p_\upeta+1)$, contain
$P_m(\tilde s,\tilde t)$, be frame invariant and be unisolvent. Beyond these
requirements it can be chosen freely: $Q_p(s,t)$ is one choice,
\cref{eq:hybrid-space} another. On non-affine elements the complement depends on
the polynomials used: $\operatorname{span}\{L_a(\xi)L_b(\eta): a+b>m\}$ and
$\operatorname{span}\{\xi^a\eta^b: a+b>m\}$ give different spaces when added to
$P_m(\tilde s,\tilde t)$. The Legendre basis is therefore part of the definition,
and \code{p.metricBasis} is ignored for \code{method=2}.

\paragraph{Straight-edged elements.} If the geometry map is bilinear and the
hybrid Vandermonde matrix is non-singular, the hybrid space equals the
parametric space, $T_p=Q_p(\xi,\eta)$: the metric functions coincide with the
parametric ones, and the element is the classical (symmetric) spectral element.
The reason is that $x$ and $y$ are bilinear in $(\xi,\eta)$ for such a map, so a
polynomial of total degree $\le m$ in $x$ and $y$, i.e.\ every function of
$P_m(\tilde s,\tilde t)$, has degree $\le m$ in $\xi$ and in $\eta$
\parencite[cf.][]{Arnold2002Approximation}. The complement lies in
$Q_p(\xi,\eta)$ by construction, and both spaces have the same dimension.

The same argument shows that the classical Lagrange spectral element on
straight-edged quadrilaterals already contains all polynomials of total degree
$\le p$ in $x$ and $y$ and passes all patch tests of degree $\le p$ under angular
distortion (verified numerically with \code{usemKeSymmetric} on the angular test
mesh of \cref{fig:test-meshes}, $p=2,3,4$). The loss of completeness under
angular distortion reported by \textcite{Lee1993effects} concerns Serendipity
elements. For full tensor-product spectral elements the unsymmetric formulation
is needed only for curved edges, and the hybrid element is unsymmetric only
there.

\paragraph{Other properties.} $T_p$ is frame invariant (both
$(\tilde s,\tilde t)$ and $(\xi,\eta)$ are), interpolatory at the nodes (so the
GLL nodal-quadrature mass matrix stays diagonal) and contains the rigid-body
modes. On the distorted test elements the Lebesgue constant stays at the
parametric level (\cref{fig:metric-basis-hybrid}): at $p=16$ it is $6.0$ instead
of $4.0\times10^7$ (trapezoid), $6.1$ instead of $5.5\times10^4$ (curved) and
$8.5$ instead of $6.7\times10^6$ (general). On curved elements the trial
functions are no longer polynomials in $(x,y)$.

\paragraph{Folded and inverted elements.} The hybrid functions are evaluated only
through $(\xi,\eta)$ (nodes at their reference coordinates, query points at
mapped reference points), so the sign of $\det(\matr J)$ is irrelevant, and
completeness holds pointwise in $(\xi,\eta)$, also where the map folds. Only
$\det(\matr J)=0$ at an evaluation point is excluded, because the gradients of the
complement contain $\matr J^{-1}$ (error
\code{ShapeFunctions2D_Metric:singularJacobian}). On the Rajendran panel meshes
with $\theta=90^\circ,\dots,270^\circ$ (all elements with $\det(\matr J)\le0$
somewhere, \code{studies/rajendran_panel}), the hybrid element passes the
tension, bending and shear patch tests for $p=3,\dots,8$, as $Q_p(s,t)$ does; the
round-off at $p=8$ is smaller ($\theta=135^\circ$: $1\times10^{-8}$ vs.\
$3\times10^{-6}$; $\theta=225^\circ$: $8\times10^{-8}$ vs.\ $2\times10^{-5}$).
Where $\det(\matr J)$ changes sign, the complement part of $\matr K_e$ has an
integrand proportional to $1/\det(\matr J)$. This does not affect the patch tests
(the complement coefficients of a field in $P_m(\tilde s,\tilde t)$ vanish), but
its effect on non-polynomial fields has not been studied.

\paragraph{Implementation.} The hybrid space is selected for a single call with
\code{ShapeFunctions2D_Metric(..., p, 2, xiQ)} and for the whole analysis with
\code{p.metricMethod=2}. The parametric coordinates of the
nodes are the reference nodes (\code{ReferenceNodes2D}). Those of the query
points are passed by all callers, which evaluate at mapped quadrature or plot
points (\code{Ke_2D}, \code{Me_2D}, \code{KMe_2D}, \code{Calc_results},
\code{Results_Stress_Strain}); otherwise they are computed by Newton's method
(\code{Global2LocalNewton}). The gradients of $P_m(\tilde s,\tilde t)$ follow
from the constant matrix $\matr J_0^{-1}$ of the skew coordinates, those of the
complement from the Jacobian at the query point,
$\nabla_{\vect x}\phi=\matr J^{-1}\nabla_{\xi}\phi$. The corresponding entries of
$\matr K_e$ therefore have rational integrands, as for curved isoparametric
elements. This does not affect the patch tests: for
$\vect u\in P_m(\tilde s,\tilde t)$ the interpolant is exact,
$\matr B_\mathrm{R}\hat{\vect u}=\bm\varepsilon(\vect u)$, and the integrand
$\det(\matr J)\,\matr B_\mathrm{L}^{\T}\matr C\,\bm\varepsilon(\vect u)$ is the
same polynomial as for \code{method=1}.

\paragraph{Mesh-level comparison} (\code{study_hybrid_space.m}). The classical
element, $Q_p(s,t)$ and the hybrid element are compared on the $3\times2$ mesh of
\cref{fig:test-meshes} (\code{PatchTest.m}) with curved-edge distortion $0.15$
and $0.20$ (minimum of $\det(\matr J)$: $0.16$ and $0.09$) and with angular plus
curved-edge distortion $0.3$ (minimum of $\det(\matr J)$: $0.014$), $p=1$--$10$
(\cref{fig:metric-hybrid-mesh}; data in
\code{docs/figures/data/metric-hybrid-*.dat}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Patch test} (harmonic field of degree $p$). The classical element
    fails on all three meshes ($10^{-2}$--$10^{-7}$). Both unsymmetric elements are
    exact; the error of $Q_p(s,t)$ grows with $p$ to $10^{-13}$--$10^{-12}$, that of
    the hybrid element stays at $\le10^{-14}$.
  \item \textbf{Smooth field} $e^x\cos(y)$. All three elements converge
    exponentially. Up to $p\approx8$, $Q_p(s,t)$ is the most accurate (by a factor
    $2$--$20$ over the hybrid element, e.g.\ curved $0.15$, $p=7$:
    $6.0\times10^{-10}$ vs.\ $1.0\times10^{-8}$); the hybrid element is
    $2$--$50$ times more accurate than the classical one. At $p=9,10$ the hybrid
    element is as accurate as or more accurate than $Q_p(s,t)$ (curved $0.20$,
    $p=10$: $3.9\times10^{-12}$ vs.\ $4.1\times10^{-10}$).
  \item \textbf{Eigenvalues} ($\vect u=\vect0$ on the boundary, consistent mass).
    The errors of the 8 eigenvalues of smallest modulus of the hybrid element
    agree with those of the classical element to two digits for every $p$
    ($4.5\times10^{-7}$ at $p=10$). $Q_p(s,t)$ produces spurious eigenvalues: from
    $p=5$--$7$ on, $2$--$25$ eigenvalues $\lambda=\omega^2$ with
    $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)<0$ and
    $|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|/|\lambda|\to1$. They reach the low end of the
    spectrum (error of the 8 lowest eigenvalues $1.9$ for curved $0.20$, $p=6$),
    and the physical eigenvalues stagnate at $10^{-5}$--$10^{-3}$. The hybrid
    element has no eigenvalue with negative real part and
    $|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|/|\lambda|\le1.7\times10^{-2}$ over the whole
    spectrum. The same holds for single elements: on the curved test elements
    $Q_p(s,t)$ gives $|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|/|\lambda|=0.1$--$0.47$ for
    $p\ge4$ and an indefinite consistent mass matrix at $p=8$, the hybrid element
    $\le7\times10^{-3}$ and a positive mass spectrum.
\end{itemize}
An eigenvalue $\omega^2$ with negative real part is an exponentially growing mode
for any time integrator. For transient, modal and frequency-domain analyses
with $p\gtrsim5$ on curved meshes the hybrid element should therefore be used. For
static analyses at moderate $p$, $Q_p(s,t)$ is slightly more accurate. Both
elements are distortion immune; neither result is covered by a stability theory
(discrete inf-sup constant), which remains open.
