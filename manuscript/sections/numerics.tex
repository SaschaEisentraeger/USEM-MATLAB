\section{Numerical studies}
\label{sec:numerics}
%
In this section, the two improvements are put to the test. Three formulations are compared: \PP\ (conventional spectral element), \PM\ (unsymmetric element with the trial space $\Qsk$ in the Lagrange basis of \cref{sec:basis}) and \PH\ (unsymmetric element with the hybrid trial space $\Tp_p$ of \cref{sec:hybrid}). Since the shape functions do not depend on the basis (\cref{sec:basis-invariance}), the trial functions of \PM\ are those of the companion paper, which uses monomials, up to round-off. The studies proceed from single elements to meshes. First, the conditioning of the Vandermonde matrix, the accuracy of the computed shape functions, the Lebesgue constants and the deviation from the conventional element are examined on four single test elements and on three one-parameter families of distorted elements (\cref{sec:num-elements,sec:num-conditioning,sec:num-lebesgue,sec:num-deviation}). Thereafter, patch tests are carried out on three distorted $3\times2$ meshes and on the folded panel meshes of Rajendran~\cite{Rajendran2010technique} (\cref{sec:num-patch}). The convergence under mesh refinement is studied on a unit square whose element shapes do not change under refinement (\cref{sec:num-convergence}), and the eigenvalues and the transient response are computed on the $3\times2$ meshes (\cref{sec:num-spectra}). After a comparison of the cost (\cref{sec:num-cost}), the thick cylinder under internal pressure serves as a last example with curved boundaries and non-polynomial solutions (\cref{sec:num-cylinder}).

All computations use the \texttt{MATLAB} implementation of the unsymmetric spectral element (USEM code base) in IEEE double precision and the scripts in \texttt{manuscript/studies}. All studies were computed with \texttt{MATLAB} R2026a, and the mesh-level studies use the calibrated Gauss rules of \cref{sec:num-quadrature}. If not stated otherwise, GLL nodes, the same degree $p$ in both directions and an isotropic material with Poisson's ratio $\nu=0.3$ in plane stress are used. The geometry is generated from 8-node (Serendipity) input elements and lifted to degree $p$, so that the element maps are at most quadratic ($q\le2$).
%
\subsection{Quadrature}
\label{sec:num-quadrature}
%
The stiffness integrands of the three formulations are not polynomials of low degree. Those of \PP\ and of the complement of \PH\ contain $1/\det(\matr J)$ (\cref{sec:hybrid-gradients}), and those of \PM\ are polynomials of a high degree in the reference coordinates. In order to compare the formulations and not their quadrature errors, the number of Gauss points per direction, $\nG$, is chosen separately for every formulation, degree and mesh such that the element matrices are integrated to round-off, as in the companion paper~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}. To this end, the element matrices are computed with $\nG=p+1,p+2,\dots$ points and compared with a reference computed with $\max(64,8p)$ points. Here, the reference rule is larger than the rule $\max(40,8p)$ of the companion paper, since 40 points are not sufficient on the nearly degenerate elements of the $3\times2$ mesh with angular and curved-edge distortion. The rule used in the studies is the smallest $\nG$ for which this and the next rule agree with the reference to a relative deviation below $10^{-12}$ in the Frobenius norm, maximized over the elements of the mesh. For the dynamic studies, the consistent mass matrix is included. For \PM\ of high degree, the element matrices themselves carry round-off errors above $10^{-12}$, of the order of $\cond(\matr V)\,u$ (\cref{sec:basis-invariance}). In these cases, the tolerance is raised to ten times the deviation between two reference rules, which amounts to at most $2.2\cdot10^{-11}$ ($p=10$, angular and curved-edge distortion). The calibration uses all elements of the $3\times2$ meshes and all elements of the meshes with $N=2,4,8$ of \cref{sec:num-convergence} (from $N=4$ on, the element shapes repeat exactly). For the thick cylinder, it uses all elements for $N\le2$ and the elements at the two arcs and in the ring closest to $r=2$ for $N=4,8$. On the unit square, the errors are integrated with $\nG+4$ points per direction. On the thick cylinder, the loads are integrated with $\max(3p+2,\nG)$ and the errors with $\max(3p+2,\nG+4)$ points per direction.

The resulting rules are compiled in \cref{tab:quadrule}. We observe that \PM\ requires $2p+1$ to $2p+4$ points on the meshes with the 8-node geometry, which is consistent with its polynomial integrand. On the thick cylinder, whose elements at the arcs have a geometry of degree $p$, it requires up to $21$ points for $p=8$. \PP\ and \PH\ require considerably more points, namely $11$--$25$ on the square meshes and up to $51$ on the $3\times2$ mesh with angular and curved-edge distortion, whose smallest Jacobian determinant is $0.014$. The former default rule of the code base, $2p\times2p$ points, therefore gave element matrices with relative errors of up to $5.4\cdot10^{-2}$ on that mesh, $1.1\cdot10^{-2}$ on the square mesh with $\delta=0.2$ and $3.8\cdot10^{-2}$ on the thick cylinder with $\alpha=30^\circ$. Note that the patch tests are not affected. For a polynomial solution of degree $p$ on the 8-node geometry, the integrand $\bar{\matr B}_{\mathrm N}^\top\matr C\,\bm\varepsilon(\vect u)$ of the unsymmetric elements is a polynomial of degree at most $3p-1$ in each reference coordinate, since the entries of $\bar{\matr B}_{\mathrm N}$ are of degree $p+1$ and those of $\bm\varepsilon(\vect u)$ of degree $2(p-1)$. Thus, every rule with at least $3p/2$ points satisfies condition (C3) for them. The single-element quantity $d_{\mathrm{K}}$ of \cref{tab:deviation} is unchanged to the digits given when the calibrated rules are used.
% data: data/quadrature/deviation-check.dat (studies/ms_deviation_check.m)

On folded elements, i.e., on the square meshes with $\delta=0.3$ and on the thick-cylinder meshes with $\alpha=45^\circ$ ($N\ge2$) and $\alpha\ge60^\circ$, no rule converges for \PP\ and \PH, since their stiffness integrals do not exist there (\cref{sec:hybrid-gradients}). The matrices computed by Gauss quadrature depend on the rule: with $2p$ points, they deviate from those with the reference rule by at least $27\,\%$ and in most cases by more than $100\,\%$. The rule of \PM, in contrast, converges. Results of \PP\ and \PH\ on folded meshes are therefore computed with the reference rule and reported only for completeness. They do not measure the accuracy of these elements.

\subsection{Test elements}
\label{sec:num-elements}
%
The element-level studies use four single elements, which are depicted in \cref{fig:elements}(a)--(d): a parallelogram (affine map), a trapezoid (straight edges, bilinear map), an element with two curved edges (\emph{curved}) and an element with angular and curved-edge distortion (\emph{general}). Their 8-node coordinates are listed in \cref{app:elements}. In addition, three one-parameter families of elements on the reference square $\Kh$ are used to separate the effect of the distortion from that of the degree (\cref{fig:elements}(e)--(g)). The first is a \emph{bent} element, whose mid-side nodes of the bottom and the top edge are moved by $c$ in the $y$-direction, so that $x=\xi$, $y=\eta+c\,(1-\xi^2)$ and $\det(\matr J)\equiv1$. The second is an element with one curved edge, whose mid-side node of the bottom edge is moved by $c$ in the $y$-direction, and the third is a trapezoid, whose top corners are moved inwards by $c$ (bilinear map). Since the edges of $\Kh$ have the length 2, the relative sagitta \cref{eq:delta} of the curved edges is $\delta=c/2$.

\subsection{Conditioning of the Vandermonde matrix and accuracy of the shape functions}
\label{sec:num-conditioning}
%
\Cref{fig:kappa} shows the condition number $\cond_2(\matr V)$ for the four bases of $\Qsk$ and for the hybrid trial space. \Cref{fig:kron} depicts the error of the Kronecker property of the computed shape functions, $e_{\mathrm{K}}=\max_{i,j}|M_i^K(\bx_j)-\delta_{ij}|$, i.e., the error of the shape functions at the nodes, which is the error for unit nodal data (\cref{sec:basis-distorted}).

On the parallelogram, we observe that the condition number for monomials grows from $2.9\cdot10^{2}$ ($p=4$) over $2.9\cdot10^{5}$ ($p=8$) to $3.5\cdot10^{11}$ ($p=16$), by a factor of about $5.8\approx(1+\sqrt2)^2$ per degree, which is the square of the one-dimensional growth. Legendre polynomials give $\cond_2(\matr V)=10.2$, $24.4$ and $55.3$, Chebyshev polynomials $\cond_2(\matr V)\le3$, and the Lagrange basis yields $\cond_2(\matr V)=1$ for every $p$, as expected from \cref{sec:basis-affine}. Accordingly, $e_{\mathrm{K}}$ grows with $p$ as $\cond_2(\matr V)\,u$ for the monomials ($2.0\cdot10^{-12}$ at $p=8$ and $1.2\cdot10^{-6}$ at $p=16$) and is at most $1.0\cdot10^{-28}$ for the Lagrange basis. The relative asymmetry $\|\matr K_K-\matr K_K^\top\|_{\mathrm{F}}/\|\matr K_K\|_{\mathrm{F}}$ of the stiffness matrix, which vanishes in exact arithmetic, follows $e_{\mathrm{K}}$ (\cref{tab:asym}). The relation \cref{eq:kappa} is reproduced to $4.1\cdot10^{-14}$ (relative) for all $p\le16$ on this element and to within the expected round-off ($\le5.2\cdot10^{-12}$) on the distorted elements.

On the trapezoid, the curved and the general element, the three bases of $[-1,1]$, used with the bounding-box scaling, lie within a factor of about 3 of one another. Compared with the monomials, they improve the condition number by a factor of 4--13 at $p=4$ and of $4.6\cdot10^{3}$--$4.5\cdot10^{4}$ at $p=16$. On the general element, e.g., $\cond_2(\matr V)=1.7\cdot10^{6}$ for monomials versus $4.7$--$8.4\cdot10^{4}$ at $p=8$ and $9.0\cdot10^{14}$ versus $0.9$--$2.0\cdot10^{11}$ at $p=16$. With monomials, the trapezoid at $p=16$ is rejected as singular to working precision. In the Lagrange basis, $e_{\mathrm{K}}$ is reduced accordingly: $8.4\cdot10^{-6}$, $6.9\cdot10^{-9}$ and $2.1\cdot10^{-6}$ on the trapezoid, the curved and the general element at $p=16$, compared with rejection, $6.0\cdot10^{-5}$ and $2.9\cdot10^{-3}$ with monomials. The remaining growth is caused by the extrapolation of the metric shape functions to the bounding box (\cref{eq:kappa}). On the general element, the second factor in \cref{eq:kappa}, $\cond_1(\matr V)/\|\matr V\|_1=\max_d\lambda_{\mathcal U}(\bm g_d)$, is $9.8\cdot10^{4}$ at $p=8$, where $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}=3.2\cdot10^{2}$, and $1.4\cdot10^{11}$ at $p=16$, where $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}=6.9\cdot10^{6}$.

The hybrid trial space poses a different interpolation problem. On the distorted elements, its Vandermonde matrix is as well conditioned as that of $\Qsk$ for $p\le3$, and from $p=4$ on it is better conditioned than that of $\Qsk$ in any basis, by a factor of $2$--$6$ at $p=4$ and of $1.6\cdot10^{3}$--$1.9\cdot10^{5}$ at $p=16$. For instance, we obtain $\cond_2(\matr V)=7.8\cdot10^{2}$ versus at least $1.3\cdot10^{5}$ on the trapezoid at $p=8$, and $1.7$--$2.0\cdot10^{6}$ versus at least $8.5\cdot10^{10}$ on the trapezoid and the general element at $p=16$. The error of the Kronecker property is $e_{\mathrm{K}}\le4.1\cdot10^{-11}$ for all elements and $p\le16$.

The Lagrange basis is always used together with the bounding-box scaling \cref{eq:bbox}. In \cref{tab:scaling}, the two effects are separated. Without it, we obtain $\cond_2(\matr V)=2.2\cdot10^{15}$ instead of $8.6\cdot10^{11}$ on the trapezoid at $p=16$, which is no better than with monomials. On the curved element, whose nodes lie in $[-1,1]^2$, the scaling has no effect, and on the parallelogram it is the identity. Note that for $p\le4$ the scaling can slightly increase the condition number.

\subsection{Lebesgue constants}
\label{sec:num-lebesgue}
%
The Lebesgue constants \cref{eq:lebesgue} are evaluated on a uniform grid of $(6p+11)^2$ reference points mapped to the element. \Cref{fig:lebesgue} shows them versus the degree $p$ for the three distorted test elements, and \cref{fig:sweep} versus the distortion parameter $c$ for the three element families of \cref{fig:elements}(e)--(g).

We observe that $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}$ grows exponentially with $p$ on all distorted elements: on the trapezoid from $10.4$ ($p=4$) over $7.7\cdot10^{2}$ ($p=8$) to $3.9\cdot10^{7}$ ($p=16$), on the curved element to $5.5\cdot10^{4}$ and on the general element to $6.9\cdot10^{6}$ at $p=16$. As expected from \cref{sec:hybrid-representation}, the hybrid trial space gives $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}=\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ exactly on the trapezoid ($6.09$ at $p=16$). On the curved element, $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}\le1.07\,\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ holds for $p\ge3$ ($1.14\,\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ at $p=2$). On the general element, $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}\le1.05\,\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ for $3\le p\le10$ ($1.11\,\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ at $p=2$). For higher degrees, the ratio grows to $1.12$ ($p=12$) and $1.40$ ($p=16$, $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}=8.5$ versus $\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}=6.1$), which is still almost six orders of magnitude below $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}$ at that degree.

\Cref{fig:sweep} separates the effect of the distortion from that of the degree. For the bent element, whose Jacobian determinant is constant, $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}$ grows from the GLL value to $8.2$, $1.9\cdot10^{2}$ and $6.4\cdot10^{3}$ at $c=0.5$ for $p=4,8,12$. Consequently, the growth is not caused by a degenerating Jacobian. In contrast, $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}$ stays within $11\,\%$ of $\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ over the whole range. For the element with one curved edge, $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}=\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ (to two digits) up to $c\approx0.4$, and $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}$ grows beyond. At the extreme $c=1$, where the mid-side node is moved by half the edge length, we obtain $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}=5.7$, $65$ and $8.6\cdot10^{2}$ for $p=4,8,12$, compared with $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}=71$, $4.6\cdot10^{4}$ and $3.2\cdot10^{7}$. For the trapezoid, $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}=\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ exactly for all $c$ (\cref{sec:hybrid-representation}), whereas $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}$ reaches $1.4\cdot10^{11}$ at $c=0.855$ for $p=12$, and $\Qp_{12}(\bar\xi,\bar\eta)$ is rejected as singular to working precision at $c=0.9$. The condition numbers of the Vandermonde matrices behave alike, e.g., $\cond_2(\matr V)=1.1\cdot10^{12}$ for $\Qsk$ in the Lagrange basis and $4.8\cdot10^{6}$ for $\Tp_p$ on the trapezoid with $p=12$ and $c=0.81$.

\subsection{Deviation from the conventional element}
\label{sec:num-deviation}
%
According to \cref{sec:hybrid-representation}, the trial functions of both unsymmetric elements are the parametric shape functions plus a correction. \Cref{tab:deviation} quantifies this correction at the $(2p)^2$ Gauss points of $\matr K_K$, $d_{\mathrm{N}}=\max_{i,r}|M_i^K(\bx_r)-N_i(\bx_r)|$, and its effect on the stiffness matrix, $d_{\mathrm{K}}=\|\matr K_K-\matr K_K^{\text{\PP}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}/\|\matr K_K^{\text{\PP}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}$. For $\Qsk$, we observe that the correction grows exponentially with $p$, to $d_{\mathrm{N}}=6.8\cdot10^{3}$ on the trapezoid and $3.9\cdot10^{3}$ on the general element at $p=12$. That is to say, the metric shape functions deviate from the parametric ones by up to $10^{3}$--$10^{4}$ between the nodes, and $\|\matr K_K-\matr K_K^{\text{\PP}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}$ reaches $9$ (curved), $3.4\cdot10^{2}$ (general) and $7.4\cdot10^{2}$ (trapezoid) times $\|\matr K_K^{\text{\PP}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}$. For $\Tp_p$, on the other hand, the correction vanishes to round-off on the trapezoid (\cref{sec:hybrid-representation}) and stays bounded on the curved elements ($d_{\mathrm{N}}\le6.4\cdot10^{-2}$ and $d_{\mathrm{K}}\le4.2\cdot10^{-2}$ for all $p\le12$). On the curved element, it even decreases with $p$. Thus, \PH\ is a small perturbation of the conventional element, confined to the completeness defect, whose size does not grow with $p$. This explains its spectral behavior, which is close to that of the conventional element (\cref{sec:num-spectra}).

\subsection{Patch tests}
\label{sec:num-patch}
%
The first mesh-level example consists of three $3\times2$ meshes on $[0,3]\times[0,2]$, which are depicted in \cref{fig:mesh32}: two with curved interior edges ($\delta=0.15$ and $0.20$) and one with angular and curved-edge distortion (\cref{app:elements}). The smallest value of $\det(\matr J)$ in the elements is $0.16$, $0.09$ and $0.014$, respectively, compared with $0.25$ for the undistorted mesh. The meshes are loaded by prescribing the harmonic field $\vect u=\bigl(\operatorname{Re}(z^p),-\operatorname{Im}(z^p)\bigr)$ with $z=(x-1.3)+\mathrm i\,(y-0.7)$ at all boundary nodes. This field is an equilibrium solution of total degree $p$ without body force. Therefore, both unsymmetric elements are expected to reproduce it exactly, whereas \PP\ is expected to fail on the curved elements (\cref{sec:completeness}). \Cref{tab:patch} lists the largest relative nodal error. As expected, both unsymmetric elements pass for every $p$, and \PP\ fails. The round-off level of \PM\ grows with $p$ to $1.1\cdot10^{-13}$--$6.9\cdot10^{-13}$ at $p=10$, whereas that of \PH\ stays at or below $2.5\cdot10^{-15}$ (cf.\ \cref{sec:basis-distorted}).

\subsection{Convergence on meshes with persistent distortion}
\label{sec:num-convergence}
%
In order to study the convergence under mesh refinement in the worst case for the conventional element (\cref{sec:completeness}), the unit square is discretized by $N\times N$ elements, $N=2,4,8,16$ (\cref{fig:square} and \cref{app:elements}). Their interior mid-side nodes are moved normal to their edge by $\delta h$, so that the relative sagitta \cref{eq:delta} of every interior edge is $\delta$. Thus, the element shapes are the same on every mesh, and the distortion does not vanish under refinement. For $\delta=0.1$ and $0.2$, all elements are valid, with the smallest $\det(\matr J)$ at the quadrature points being $0.20$ and $0.095$, compared with $0.25$ for the undistorted mesh. For $\delta=0.3$, the elements fold near two corners, i.e., $\det(\matr J)<0$ at quadrature points. The exact solution $\vect u=\bigl(\operatorname{Re}(f),-\operatorname{Im}(f)\bigr)$ with $f(z)=e^{2z}$ and $z=x+\mathrm i\,y$ is an equilibrium field without body force and is prescribed at all boundary nodes. The relative energy-norm error $\eE=\enormh{\vect u-\vect u_h}/\enorm{\vect u}$ is evaluated with the strains of the trial functions of each formulation (\cref{sec:num-quadrature}). On the folded meshes, the energy integral contains $1/\det(\matr J)$ and is not meaningful, and the largest relative nodal error $e_{\mathrm{n}}$ is used instead. The errors are depicted in \cref{fig:convergence}, and the observed rates are compiled in \cref{tab:rates}.

Due to the persistent distortion, \PP\ is expected to converge with reduced rates. We observe rates far below $p$, of the order of $p/2$ ($r_{8,16}=0.89$, $0.82$, $1.84$, $1.72$ and $2.80$ for $p=2,\dots,6$ at $\delta=0.2$), which is consistent with the complete degree $\lfloor p/2\rfloor$ of the mapped shape functions (\cref{sec:completeness}) and with the rates observed in the companion paper. On the finest mesh, \PP\ is one to four and a half orders of magnitude less accurate than the stable unsymmetric elements.

For the moderate distortion $\delta=0.1$, both unsymmetric elements converge with the optimal rate $p$ for all $p\le6$. \PM\ is, however, the more accurate one, and the ratio $e_{\mathrm{E}}^{\text{\PH}}/e_{\mathrm{E}}^{\text{\PM}}$ on the finest mesh grows from $1.3$ ($p=2$) to $5.6$ ($p=6$). This is the price of the hybrid trial space for smooth solutions. Here, $\Qsk$ contains Cartesian polynomials up to the total degree $2p$, whereas $\Tp_p$ contains them only up to $p$, so that the best fit in \cref{eq:quasiopt} is smaller for $\Qsk$.

For the strong distortion $\delta=0.2$, the picture is the same for $p\le4$, with ratios of $2$--$5$ in favor of \PM\ and optimal rates for both. For $p=5$ and $6$, however, \PM\ \emph{diverges} under refinement. For $p=5$, its error grows from $5.5\cdot10^{-7}$ ($N=4$) to $5.9\cdot10^{-3}$ ($N=16$), and on the finest mesh it is even larger than that of \PP. In contrast, \PH\ converges with $r_{8,16}=4.97$ and $5.98$ and reaches $3.1\cdot10^{-9}$ and $2.9\cdot10^{-11}$, i.e., about six orders of magnitude less than \PM. Since the elements are the same on all meshes, the element-level quantities (Lebesgue constants and condition numbers) do not change with $N$, and by \cref{eq:interp} the interpolation error of the trial functions of \PM\ decreases with the optimal order. Therefore, the divergence of \PM\ cannot be attributed to the best-fit term of \cref{eq:quasiopt}. Instead, it indicates that the stability factor $\beta_h$ of the assembled unsymmetric system decreases as the number of elements grows. This is consistent with the spurious eigenvalues of \cref{sec:num-spectra} and with the observations for the thick cylinder in \cref{sec:num-cylinder}.

On the folded meshes ($\delta=0.3$), only \PM\ has well-defined stiffness matrices (\cref{sec:num-quadrature}). It converges in the nodal error for $p=2,3$ (e.g., $3.5\cdot10^{-7}$ for $p=3$ and $N=16$), but diverges for $p\ge4$, with nodal errors of up to $45$ at $p=5$ and $N=16$. The results of \PP\ and \PH\ depend on the quadrature rule. With the $2p$ rule, both converged in the nodal error, \PH\ for $p=5$, e.g., to $4.9\cdot10^{-10}$ on the finest mesh. With the reference rule, however, \PH\ diverges for $p=2$, with a nodal error of $25$ at $N=16$, and stagnates at $3.8\cdot10^{-7}$ for $p=5$, and the nodal error of \PP\ on the finest mesh is $6$ to $45$ times larger for $p=3$ and $5$. These computations therefore allow no statement on the accuracy of \PP\ and \PH\ on folded meshes.

\subsection{Eigenvalues and transient stability}
\label{sec:num-spectra}
%
A loss of stability of the unsymmetric system shows up most clearly in the eigenvalues and in the transient response. Therefore, the complete spectrum of the generalized eigenvalue problem $\matr K\bm\phi=\lambda\matr M\bm\phi$ with $\lambda=\omega^2$ is computed for the three $3\times2$ meshes of \cref{fig:mesh32} with $\vect u=\vect0$ on the boundary and the consistent mass matrix ($\rho=1$). For the conventional element, all eigenvalues are real and positive. \Cref{tab:spectrum} gives the number of eigenvalues with $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)<0$ and the largest relative imaginary part $\max|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|/|\lambda|$, and \cref{fig:spectrum} depicts the largest relative error of the real parts of the 8 eigenvalues of smallest modulus. Here, the conventional element on the undistorted mesh with $p=12$ serves as reference. We observe that \PM\ produces eigenvalues with $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)<0$ from $p=5$ (curved $0.20$, angular + curved $0.30$) or $p=7$ (curved $0.15$) on, up to 25 of them at $p=10$, with $|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|/|\lambda|\to1$. These eigenvalues enter the low end of the spectrum. Consequently, the error of the lowest eigenvalues jumps to $1.9$ (curved $0.20$, $p=6$) and $0.35$ (angular + curved $0.30$, $p=7$) and stagnates at $10^{-5}$--$10^{-3}$ for higher degrees. The error of \PP, on the other hand, decreases to $4.4$--$4.5\cdot10^{-7}$ at $p=10$, a value that is common to all meshes and formulations without spurious modes. It reflects the accuracy of the reference values and the corner singularities of the clamped rectangle, so that differences below this level are not significant. \PH, in contrast, has no eigenvalue with negative real part on any mesh and for any $p$, and $|\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)|/|\lambda|\le1.7\cdot10^{-2}$ holds over the whole spectrum. The error of its lowest eigenvalues is $0.32$ to $1.07$ times that of \PP\ for every mesh and $p$ (\cref{fig:spectrum}), whereas the error of \PM\ is up to $4\cdot10^{4}$ times larger.

For the free vibration $\matr M\ddot{\vect u}+\matr K\vect u=\vect 0$, the sign of $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)$ alone is not the relevant stability measure. Every eigenvalue that is not real and positive yields solutions $e^{\pm\mathrm i\sqrt\lambda t}\bm\phi$, one of which grows with the rate $|\operatorname{Im}(\sqrt\lambda)|$. \Cref{tab:growth} lists the largest growth exponent per fundamental period $T_1=2\pi/\omega_1$ of \PP, $\mu=T_1\max|\operatorname{Im}(\sqrt\lambda)|$, and the corresponding exponent of the trapezoidal rule (Newmark method with $\beta=1/4$, $\gamma=1/2$ and $\Delta t=T_1/20$), $\mu_\Delta=20\ln\bigl(\max|\chi|\bigr)$, with the amplification factors $\chi=(1+\mathrm i\vartheta)/(1-\mathrm i\vartheta)$ and $\vartheta=\pm\sqrt\lambda\,\Delta t/2$. That is to say, a perturbation grows by the factor $e^{\mu}$ per period in the exact time integration and by $e^{\mu_\Delta}$ per period with the trapezoidal rule. \PP\ is stable ($\mu=0$). For \PM, $\mu$ grows from the order of one at $p=2$ to $10^{2}$--$10^{4}$ at $p=6,7$, and $\mu_\Delta$ reaches $5$--$33$ per period. Thus, a perturbation at the level of round-off is amplified to the order of one within a few periods. For \PH, $\mu\le0.65$ and $\mu_\Delta\le0.16$ on all meshes and for all $p$ ($\le0.11$ for $p\ge3$). Therefore, we have to admit that \PH\ is \emph{not} free of growing modes. Their growth is, however, considerably slower than that of \PM. At $p=2$, it is slower by a factor of $2.5$ (angular + curved $0.30$) and $37$ (curved $0.20$), and on the mesh with curved $0.15$ \PH\ shows no growth at all. At $p=3$, the factor amounts to $20$--$30$, and at $p=7$ to up to four orders of magnitude ($\mu$). Moreover, $\mu_\Delta$ does not increase with $p$.

\subsection{Thick cylinder}
\label{sec:num-cylinder}
%
As a last example, we use the thick cylinder under internal pressure of Rajendran~\cite[Sect.~5.9]{Rajendran2010technique} as a benchmark with curved boundaries and non-polynomial solutions. A sketch of the model including its dimensions and boundary conditions is provided in \cref{fig:cylsetup}(a), while the corresponding finite element discretizations are depicted in \cref{fig:cylsetup}(b)--(h). Due to the symmetry, a quarter annulus $1\le r\le3$ is modeled with Young's modulus $E=1000$, Poisson's ratio $\nu=0.25$ and the internal pressure $p_{\mathrm{i}}=1000$ in plane stress, with symmetry conditions on the straight edges and a traction-free outer arc. It is computed with all three formulations by the scripts of the study in \texttt{studies/thick\_cylinder} ($1512$ cases, $39$ of them rejected), here with the calibrated Gauss rules of \cref{sec:num-quadrature}. The meshes consist of $2N\times2N$ 8-node elements, $N=1,2,4,8$, generated with \texttt{Gmsh}~\cite{Geuzaine2009Gmsh}. They are lifted to degree $p=2,\dots,8$, with the arcs represented exactly by Gordon-Hall blending~\cite{Gordon1973Transfinite}. In every $2\times2$ super-element, the interior mid-side nodes are rotated by $\alpha\in\{0^\circ,15^\circ,30^\circ,45^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ\}$ (mesh sequence B of Rajendran~\cite{Rajendran2010technique}), so that the relative distortion is the same on every mesh and the element maps do not approach affine maps under refinement. Elements fold for $\alpha=45^\circ$ ($N\ge2$) and $\alpha\ge60^\circ$. Three fields are computed, namely the Lam\'e solution, a manufactured polynomial of total degree 5 and the analytic field $u_x=\tfrac1{10}(\sin(3X)\cos(2Y)+e^{XY})$, $u_y=\tfrac1{10}(\cos(2X)\sin(3Y)+Ye^{-X})$ with $X=x/3$ and $Y=y/3$. For the latter two fields, the body forces and the tractions on both arcs are derived from the exact fields, and both displacement components are prescribed on the straight edges. The errors are measured by the relative energy-norm error $\eE$, evaluated with the strains of the respective trial functions, and by the largest relative nodal error $e_{\mathrm{n}}$. On folded meshes, only $e_{\mathrm{n}}$ is meaningful. Cases in which the interpolation matrix is singular to working precision ($\mathrm{rcond}<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$) are rejected.

For \PH, the ratio lies between $0.78$ and $1.00$ in all $158$ cases with $\alpha\le30^\circ$ and errors above round-off, and for \PP\ between $0.84$ and $1.00$ ($165$ cases). Thus, the finite element solutions of both are practically as accurate as the interpolant in this example. On the single coarse mesh with $\alpha=45^\circ$ and $N=1$, which is not folded either, the ratio ranges from $0.71$ to $1.60$ for \PH\ and from $0.70$ to $0.93$ for \PP. For \PM, the ratio ranges from $0.08$ to $10^5$. At $\alpha=30^\circ$ and $p\ge3$, the \PM\ solution is up to $300$ times worse \emph{or} up to five times better than its interpolant, i.e., it does not inherit the approximation property of its trial space. This is consistent with the loss of stability observed in \cref{sec:num-convergence}.

\Cref{tab:cyllebesgue} lists the Lebesgue constants and condition numbers, maximized over the elements of the finest mesh. We observe that the Lebesgue constant of \PM\ reaches $10^3$ at $30^\circ$ and $p=4$ and $1.3\cdot10^8$ at $45^\circ$ and $p=8$. In contrast, that of \PH\ stays within $30\,\%$ of the GLL value up to $15^\circ$ and at or below $95$ at $30^\circ$. The condition number $\cond_2(\matr V)$ of \PM\ exceeds its Lebesgue constant by up to two orders of magnitude at $30^\circ$ and almost four at $45^\circ$, which is the extrapolation to the bounding box of \cref{eq:kappa}. In the study of the code base with the former $2p$ rule, the global stiffness matrices were analyzed as well. In four of the five \PM\ cases tested with $\alpha\ge30^\circ$, a single one of the six lowest triplets carries $21$--$68\,\%$ of the error. \Cref{fig:cylinder} shows $e_{\mathrm{n}}$ on the finest mesh versus $\alpha$. On the folded meshes ($\alpha\ge45^\circ$), only \PM\ has well-defined stiffness matrices (\cref{sec:num-quadrature}). Its nodal error there is erratic and in most cases larger than on the valid meshes, e.g., $1.1\cdot10^{-2}$ at $\alpha=45^\circ$ and $p=8$, and it is rejected for $p\ge6$ at $\alpha=60^\circ$. The values of \PP\ and \PH\ for $\alpha\ge45^\circ$ depend on the quadrature rule. At $\alpha=90^\circ$ and $p=6$, e.g., the nodal error of \PH\ is $7.0\cdot10^{-6}$ with the reference rule and $2.0\cdot10^{-7}$ with the $2p$ rule, and that of \PP\ is $7.2\cdot10^{-5}$ and $1.1\cdot10^{-4}$, respectively. In \cref{fig:cylinder} and \cref{tab:cylinder}, they are therefore given only for completeness. The existence of the hybrid shape functions, on the other hand, does not depend on the quadrature. For $p\ge7$ at $\alpha=60^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, the Vandermonde matrix of the hybrid trial space is singular to working precision on some folded elements. The results in \cref{tab:unisolvence} confirm \cref{eq:factor}: $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr S)$ follows $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr V_{\mathrm{T}})$ within a factor of $30$ in all cases with regular matrices, and the singular cases are exactly those with $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr S)\le4\cdot10^{-16}$, while all other cases have $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr S)\ge2\cdot10^{-15}$. Therefore, the hybrid trial space is not a remedy for folded elements: its stiffness integrals do not exist there, and at high degree its Vandermonde matrix may be singular to working precision. This singularity is caused by the skew coordinates, not by the trial space itself (\cref{app:frames}).
