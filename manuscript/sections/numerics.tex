\section{Numerical studies}
\label{sec:numerics}

All computations use the MATLAB/GNU Octave implementation of the unsymmetric spectral
element (USEM code base) in IEEE double precision; the data were produced with GNU
Octave~8.4 by the scripts in \texttt{manuscript/studies}. Unless stated otherwise:
GLL nodes, $p_\xi=p_\eta=p$, isotropic material with $\nu=0.3$ (plane stress),
$2p\times2p$ Gauss points for $\matr K_e$ and the consistent mass matrix. The
geometry is generated from 8-node (serendipity) input elements and lifted to degree
$p$, so the element maps are at most biquadratic ($g\le2$). The three formulations
compared are \PP\ (classical spectral element), \PM\ (metric trial space
$\Qp_p(s,t)$, Lagrange basis, \cref{sec:basis}) and \PH\ (hybrid trial space
$\Tp_p$, \cref{sec:hybrid}).

\subsection{Test elements and node geometry}
\label{sec:num-elements}

Four single elements are used: a parallelogram (affine), a trapezoid (straight
edges, bilinear map), an element with two curved edges (``curved'') and an element
with angular and curved-edge distortion (``general''); their 8-node coordinates are
listed in \cref{app:elements}. \Cref{fig:nodes} shows the GLL nodes of the general
element for $p=8$ in the three coordinate systems involved. In the scaled skew
coordinates, in which $\Qp_p(s,t)$ interpolates, the nodes form a visibly perturbed
tensor grid; in the reference coordinates, in which the complement $\Cp_p$ of the
hybrid space interpolates, they form the exact GLL grid.

\subsection{Conditioning of the Vandermonde matrix and accuracy of the shape functions}
\label{sec:num-conditioning}

\Cref{fig:kappa} shows $\cond_2(\matr V)$ for the four bases of $\Qp_p(s,t)$ and for
the hybrid space; \cref{fig:kron} shows the error of the Kronecker property of the
computed shape functions, $e_\delta=\max_{l,k}|M_l^e(\bx_k)-\delta_{lk}|$, which is the
error for unit nodal data (\cref{rem:reproduction}).

\paragraph{Affine element} With monomials, $\cond_2(\matr V)$ grows from
$2.9\cdot10^{2}$ ($p=4$) over $2.9\cdot10^{5}$ ($p=8$) to $3.5\cdot10^{11}$ ($p=16$),
i.e.\ by a factor of about $5.8\approx(1+\sqrt2)^2$ per degree, the square of the 1D
growth. Legendre polynomials give $\cond_2=10.2$, $24.4$, $55.3$, Chebyshev
polynomials $\cond_2\le3$, and the Lagrange basis $\cond_2=1$ for every $p$
(\cref{cor:affine}). Accordingly, $e_\delta$ grows with $p$ as $\cond_2(\matr V)u$ for
the monomials ($2.0\cdot10^{-12}$ at $p=8$, $1.1\cdot10^{-6}$ at $p=16$) and is at most
$1.1\cdot10^{-28}$ for the Lagrange basis. The asymmetry
$\|\matr K_e-\matr K_e^\T\|_F/\|\matr K_e\|_F$, zero in exact arithmetic, follows
$e_\delta$:

The identity of \cref{prop:kappa} is reproduced to $4.1\cdot10^{-14}$ (relative) for
all $p\le16$ on this element, and to within the expected round-off ($\le1.8\cdot10^{-11}$)
on the distorted elements.

\paragraph{Distorted elements} On the trapezoid, the curved and the general element,
the three bases of $[-1,1]$ (with bounding-box scaling) lie within a factor of
about~3 of one another; they improve on the monomials by a factor of 4--13 at $p=4$
and of $4.6\cdot10^{3}$--$4.5\cdot10^{4}$ at $p=16$ (general element: $\cond_2=1.7\cdot10^{6}$ versus $4.7$--$8.4\cdot10^{4}$ at
$p=8$, $9.0\cdot10^{14}$ versus $0.9$--$2.0\cdot10^{11}$ at $p=16$). With monomials
the trapezoid at $p=16$ is rejected as singular to working precision. In the Lagrange
basis $e_\delta$ is reduced accordingly (trapezoid, curved and general element at
$p=16$: $7.1\cdot10^{-6}$, $6.0\cdot10^{-9}$ and $1.8\cdot10^{-6}$, versus rejection,
$4.9\cdot10^{-5}$ and $6.5\cdot10^{-4}$ with monomials). The remaining growth is caused by
the extrapolation of the metric interpolant to the bounding box (\cref{prop:kappa}): the
factor $\cond_1(\matr V)/\|\matr V\|_1=\max_d\lambda_{\mathcal U}(\hat{\vect s}_d)$ is
$9.8\cdot10^{4}$ on the general element at $p=8$, where $\Lam_Q=3.2\cdot10^{2}$, and
$1.4\cdot10^{11}$ at $p=16$, where $\Lam_Q=6.9\cdot10^{6}$. The hybrid space has a different
interpolation problem; its Vandermonde matrix is as well conditioned as that of
$\Qp_p(s,t)$ for $p\le3$ and better conditioned in any basis from $p=4$ on, by a factor
of $2$--$6$ at $p=4$ and of $1.6\cdot10^{3}$--$1.9\cdot10^{5}$ at $p=16$: $\cond_2=7.8\cdot10^{2}$ versus $\ge1.3\cdot10^{5}$ (trapezoid, $p=8$) and
$1.7$--$2.0\cdot10^{6}$ versus $\ge8.5\cdot10^{10}$ (trapezoid and general element,
$p=16$), with $e_\delta\le3.2\cdot10^{-11}$ for all elements and $p\le16$.

\subsection{Lebesgue constants}
\label{sec:num-lebesgue}

The Lebesgue constants are evaluated on a uniform grid of $(6p+11)^2$ reference
points mapped to the element. \Cref{fig:lebesgue} shows them versus $p$ for the three
distorted test elements, \cref{fig:sweep} versus the amount of distortion $\delta$ for
three one-parameter families on $\hat\Omega$: a ``bent'' element (mid-side nodes of the
bottom and top edge moved by $\delta$ in $y$, so that $x=\xi$,
$y=\eta+\delta(1-\xi^2)$ and $\det\matr J\equiv1$), an element with one curved edge,
and a trapezoid (top corners moved inwards by $\delta$; bilinear).

\paragraph{Dependence on the degree} On all distorted elements $\Lam_Q$ grows
exponentially with $p$: on the trapezoid from $10.4$ ($p=4$) over $7.7\cdot10^{2}$
($p=8$) to $3.9\cdot10^{7}$ ($p=16$), on the curved element to $5.5\cdot10^{4}$ and on
the general element to $6.9\cdot10^{6}$ at $p=16$. The hybrid space gives
$\Lam_T=\Lam_N$ exactly on the trapezoid (\cref{cor:defect}; $6.09$ at $p=16$) and
$\Lam_T\le1.07\,\Lam_N$ for $p\ge3$ on the curved element ($1.14\,\Lam_N$ at $p=2$). On the
general element $\Lam_T\le1.05\,\Lam_N$ for $3\le p\le10$ ($1.11\,\Lam_N$ at $p=2$), and the
ratio grows for higher $p$ to $1.12$ ($p=12$) and $1.40$ ($p=16$, $\Lam_T=8.5$ versus
$\Lam_N=6.1$), almost six orders of magnitude below $\Lam_Q$ at that degree.

\paragraph{Dependence on the distortion} \Cref{fig:sweep} separates the effect of the
distortion from that of the degree. For the bent element, whose Jacobian determinant is
constant, $\Lam_Q$ grows from the GLL value to $8.2$, $1.9\cdot10^{2}$ and
$6.4\cdot10^{3}$ at $\delta=0.5$ for $p=4,8,12$, so the growth is not caused by a
degenerating Jacobian. $\Lam_T$ stays within $11\,\%$ of $\Lam_N$ over the whole range.
For the element with one curved edge, $\Lam_T=\Lam_N$ (to two digits) up to
$\delta\approx0.4$, and grows beyond; at the extreme $\delta=1$ (mid-side node moved by
half the edge length) $\Lam_T=5.7$, $65$, $8.6\cdot10^{2}$ for $p=4,8,12$, compared with
$\Lam_Q=71$, $4.6\cdot10^{4}$, $3.2\cdot10^{7}$. For the trapezoid, $\Lam_T=\Lam_N$ exactly
for all $\delta$ (\cref{cor:defect}), whereas $\Lam_Q$ reaches $1.4\cdot10^{11}$ at
$\delta=0.855$, $p=12$, and $\Qp_{12}(s,t)$ is rejected as singular to working precision at
$\delta=0.9$. The condition numbers of the Vandermonde matrices behave alike
(trapezoid, $p=12$, $\delta=0.81$: $\cond_2=1.1\cdot10^{12}$ for $\Qp_p(s,t)$ in the
Lagrange basis, $4.8\cdot10^{6}$ for $\Tp_p$).

\subsection{Deviation from the classical element}
\label{sec:num-deviation}

\Cref{prop:representation} writes both metric families as the parametric shape functions
plus a correction. \Cref{tab:deviation} quantifies the correction at the $(2p)^2$ Gauss
points of $\matr K_e$, $d_N=\max_{k,q}|M_k(\bx_q)-N_k(\bx_q)|$, and its effect on the
stiffness matrix, $d_K=\|\matr K_e-\matr K_e^{\PP}\|_F/\|\matr K_e^{\PP}\|_F$. For
$\Qp_p(s,t)$ the correction grows exponentially with $p$, to $d_N=6.8\cdot10^{3}$ on the
trapezoid and $3.9\cdot10^{3}$ on the general element at $p=12$; the metric shape
functions then deviate from the parametric ones by up to $10^{3}$--$10^{4}$ between the
nodes, and $\|\matr K_e-\matr K_e^{\PP}\|_F$ reaches $9$ (curved), $3.4\cdot10^{2}$
(general) and $7.4\cdot10^{2}$ (trapezoid) times $\|\matr K_e^{\PP}\|_F$. For $\Tp_p$ the correction vanishes to round-off on the trapezoid
(\cref{cor:defect}) and stays bounded on the curved elements, $d_N\le6.4\cdot10^{-2}$
and $d_K\le4.2\cdot10^{-2}$ for all $p\le12$, decreasing with $p$ on the curved element.
\PH\ is thus a small, $p$-robust perturbation of the classical element, confined to the
completeness defect, which explains its classical-like spectral behaviour
(\cref{sec:num-spectra}).

\paragraph{Bounding-box scaling} The scaling \eqref{eq:bbox} pays off at higher degree,
where the nodes leave $[-1,1]^2$ in skew coordinates: for the Lagrange basis of
$\Qp_p(s,t)$ at $p=12$, $\cond_2=3.8\cdot10^{8}$ instead of $4.7\cdot10^{10}$ on the
trapezoid and $1.3\cdot10^{8}$ instead of $7.8\cdot10^{8}$ on the general element. On the
curved element the effect is at most a factor $1.5$, and for $p\le4$ the scaling can
slightly increase the condition number (trapezoid, $p=2$: $8.3$ instead of $5.8$).

\subsection{Patch tests}
\label{sec:num-patch}

\paragraph{Distorted meshes} The $3\times2$ meshes on $[0,3]\times[0,2]$ with
curved-edge distortion $0.15$ and $0.20$ and with combined angular and curved-edge
distortion (\cref{app:elements}; minimum $\det\matr J$ at the quadrature points $0.16$,
$0.09$ and $0.02$, relative to $0.25$ for the undistorted mesh) are loaded by prescribing the harmonic field
$\vect u=(\operatorname{Re}z^p,-\operatorname{Im}z^p)$, $z=(x-1.3)+\mathrm i(y-0.7)$, at all
boundary nodes; it is an equilibrium field of total degree $p$ without body force.
\Cref{tab:patch} lists the maximum relative nodal error. \PP\ fails, as expected
for curved edges. Both metric spaces pass for every $p$; the round-off level of \PM\
grows with $p$ to $1.2\cdot10^{-13}$--$8.1\cdot10^{-13}$ at $p=10$, whereas \PH\ stays at
$\le3.1\cdot10^{-15}$ (cf.\ \cref{rem:reproduction}).

\subsection{Convergence on meshes with persistent distortion}
\label{sec:num-convergence}

The unit square is discretised by $N\times N$ elements, $N=2,4,8,16$, whose interior
mid-side nodes are moved normal to their edge by $\delta h$ (\cref{app:elements}).
The element shapes are thus the same on every mesh: the distortion does not vanish under
refinement, which is the worst case for the classical element. For $\delta=0.1$ and
$0.2$ all elements are valid (minimum $\det\matr J$ at the quadrature points $0.20$ and
$0.095$, relative to $0.25$ for the undistorted mesh); for $\delta=0.3$ the elements fold
near two corners ($\det\matr J<0$ at quadrature points). The exact solution
$\vect u=(\operatorname{Re}f,-\operatorname{Im}f)$, $f(z)=e^{2z}$, $z=x+\mathrm iy$, is an
equilibrium field without body force and is prescribed at all boundary nodes. The relative
energy-norm error $e_E$ is evaluated with the strains of the trial functions of each
formulation and $(2p+2)^2$ Gauss points; on the folded meshes the energy integral contains
$1/\det\matr J$ and is not meaningful, and the maximum relative nodal error $e_n$ is used.
\Cref{fig:convergence} shows the errors, \cref{tab:rates} the observed rates.

\paragraph{Classical element} \PP\ converges with rates far below $p$, of the order of
$p/2$ ($r_{8,16}=0.89$, $0.82$, $1.84$, $1.72$, $2.80$ for $p=2,\dots,6$ at
$\delta=0.2$), consistent with
$\Pp_{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(x,y)\subset\Qp_p(\xi,\eta)\circ\vect F_e^{-1}$ for biquadratic maps
(\cref{sec:formulation}). On the finest mesh it is one to four and a half orders of
magnitude less accurate than the stable metric elements.

\paragraph{Moderate distortion ($\delta=0.1$)} Both metric spaces converge with the optimal
rate $p$ for all $p\le6$. \PM\ is the more accurate one: the ratio
$e_E^{\PH}/e_E^{\PM}$ on the finest mesh grows from $1.3$ ($p=2$) to $5.5$ ($p=6$). This is
the expected price of the hybrid space for smooth solutions: $\Qp_p(s,t)$ contains
Cartesian polynomials up to total degree $2p$, $\Tp_p$ only up to $p$, and the
constant of the best approximation is correspondingly smaller for $\Qp_p(s,t)$.

\paragraph{Strong distortion ($\delta=0.2$)} For $p\le4$ the picture is the same (ratio
$2$--$5$ in favour of \PM, optimal rates for both). For $p=5$ and $6$, however, \PM\
\emph{diverges} under refinement: its error grows from $5.5\cdot10^{-7}$ ($N=4$) to
$5.8\cdot10^{-3}$ ($N=16$) for $p=5$, and on the finest mesh it is larger than that of
\PP.
\PH\ converges with $r_{8,16}=4.97$ and $5.98$ and reaches $3.1\cdot10^{-9}$ and
$2.9\cdot10^{-11}$, i.e.\ six and seven orders of magnitude below \PM. Since the elements
are the same on all meshes, the element-level quantities (Lebesgue constants, condition
numbers) do not change with $N$, and by \cref{lem:interp} the interpolation error in
$\Qp_p(s,t)$ decreases with the optimal order. The divergence of \PM\ can therefore not be
attributed to approximation; it indicates a loss of stability of the assembled
Petrov--Galerkin operator as the number of elements grows. This is consistent with the spurious eigenvalues of
\cref{sec:num-spectra} and with the observations for the thick cylinder in
\cref{sec:num-cylinder}.

\paragraph{Folded elements ($\delta=0.3$)} \PM\ diverges for $p\ge4$ (nodal error up to
$44$ at $p=5$, $N=16$). \PH\ converges in the nodal error, e.g.\ $p=5$:
$8.1\cdot10^{-5}$, $1.7\cdot10^{-6}$, $3.0\cdot10^{-8}$, $4.9\cdot10^{-10}$ for
$N=2,\dots,16$, but less regularly than on the valid meshes ($p=6$ stagnates at
$4\cdot10^{-10}$ on the two finest meshes), and the energy-norm error stagnates at
$10^{-8}$ for $p\ge5$. The reason is the integrand $\propto1/\det\matr J$ of the complement
part of $\matr K_e$ (\cref{sec:hybrid-gradients}), which is singular where $\det\matr J$
changes sign and is not integrated accurately by Gauss quadrature. \PP\ converges in the
nodal error with reduced rates ($2.8\cdot10^{-6}$ for $p=5$, $N=16$).

\subsection{Eigenvalues and transient stability}
\label{sec:num-spectra}

\paragraph{Generalised eigenvalues} For the same three meshes with $\vect u=\vect0$ on
the boundary and the consistent mass matrix ($\rho=1$), the complete spectrum of
$\matr K\bm\phi=\lambda\matr M\bm\phi$, $\lambda=\omega^2$, is computed. \Cref{tab:spectrum}
gives the number of eigenvalues with $\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$ and
$\max|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|/|\lambda|$; \cref{fig:spectrum} the maximum relative error
of the real parts of the 8 eigenvalues of smallest modulus, with the classical element on
the undistorted mesh with $p=12$ as reference. \PM\ produces eigenvalues with
$\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$ from $p=5$ (curved $0.20$, angular + curved) or $p=7$
(curved $0.15$) on, up to 25 of them at $p=10$, with
$|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|/|\lambda|\to1$. They enter the low end of the spectrum: the
error of the lowest eigenvalues jumps to $1.9$ (curved $0.20$, $p=6$) and $0.35$
(angular + curved, $p=7$) and stagnates at $10^{-5}$--$10^{-3}$ for higher $p$,
whereas the error of \PP\ decreases to a plateau of $4.4$--$4.5\cdot10^{-7}$ for
$p\ge8$, common to all meshes and formulations without spurious modes, which reflects the
accuracy of the reference values (classical element, $p=12$) and the corner
singularities of the clamped rectangle; differences below this level are not
significant. \PH\ has no
eigenvalue with negative real part on any mesh and for any $p$,
$|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|/|\lambda|\le1.7\cdot10^{-2}$ over the whole spectrum, and the
error of its lowest eigenvalues is between $0.32$ and $1.07$ times that of \PP\ for every
mesh and $p$ (\cref{fig:spectrum}); the error of \PM\ is up to $4\cdot10^{4}$ times larger.

\paragraph{Growth rates and transient response} The relevant stability measure for
$\matr M\ddot{\vect u}+\matr K\vect u=\vect 0$ is not the sign of
$\operatorname{Re}\lambda$ alone: every eigenvalue that is not real and positive yields
solutions $e^{\pm\mathrm i\sqrt\lambda t}\bm\phi$, one of which grows with the rate
$|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt\lambda|$. \Cref{tab:growth} lists the largest growth exponent per
fundamental period $T_1=2\pi/\omega_1$ (of \PP),
$g=T_1\max|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt\lambda|$, and the corresponding exponent of the
trapezoidal rule (Newmark, $\beta=1/4$, $\gamma=1/2$, $\Delta t=T_1/20$),
$g_\Delta=20\ln\max|z|$ with the amplification factors
$z=(1+\mathrm ic)/(1-\mathrm ic)$, $c=\pm\sqrt\lambda\,\Delta t/2$. \PP\ is stable
($g=0$). For \PM, $g$ grows from $\mathcal O(1)$ at $p=2$ to $10^{2}$--$10^{4}$ at
$p=6,7$, and $g_\Delta$ reaches $5$--$33$ per period: a perturbation at round-off level is
amplified to $\mathcal O(1)$ within a few periods. For \PH, $g\le0.65$ and
$g_\Delta\le0.16$ on all meshes and for all $p$ ($\le0.11$ for $p\ge3$). \PH\ is therefore
\emph{not} free of growing modes, but their growth is slower than that of \PM\ by a factor
of $2$--$3$ at $p=2$, $10$--$30$ at $p=3$ and up to four orders of magnitude ($g$) at
$p=7$, and $g_\Delta$ does not increase with $p$.

\Cref{fig:transient} shows the free vibration from a smooth initial displacement,
$\vect u_0=(\sin\frac{\pi x}{3}\sin\frac{\pi y}{2},\ \sin\frac{2\pi x}{3}\sin\frac{\pi y}{2})$,
perturbed by $10^{-10}$ (relative) to emulate round-off, over $50\,T_1$ with the trapezoidal
rule and $\Delta t=T_1/20$. For \PM\ the response grows exponentially and exceeds $10^{29}$
(the computation is stopped at $10^{30}$) for every $p=4,\dots,8$ on both meshes, including
$p=4$, where all eigenvalues have positive real part. \PH\ and \PP\ remain bounded
($\max|\vect u(t)|/\max|\vect u_0|\le1.81$ for all $p$), and their responses are
indistinguishable in the plot; over $50\,T_1$ the residual growth of \PH\ amplifies the
round-off perturbation by at most $e^{50g_\Delta}\le e^{4.4}$ ($p=4,\dots,8$), which is invisible at
the scale of the response. For very long integration times, or for time steps that resolve
the growing high-frequency modes, this residual growth of \PH\ would eventually become
visible; a dissipative integrator or an energy-consistent formulation
\citep{Wang2022Temporal,Zaehringer2026On} would be needed to remove it.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:num-cost}

Run times of the reference implementation (GNU Octave~8.4, one core, median of seven
runs, general element) are listed in \cref{tab:timing}. The Lagrange basis costs
$1.0$--$1.35$ times as much as the monomials. The hybrid shape functions are $2$--$35$
times more expensive to evaluate in this implementation, because the Jacobian at the query
points is recomputed from the parametric shape-function derivatives and the basis matrix is
assembled column by column; both are implementation details, not inherent costs, since the
element routines already have $\matr J(\bxi_q)$. At the level of the element stiffness
matrix, which is dominated by the quadrature loop, the overhead of \PH\ is $7$--$46\,\%$
over the classical element and $18$--$33\,\%$ over \PM\ ($p=2,\dots,12$); the solution of the unsymmetric
global system (LU instead of Cholesky) is the same for \PM\ and \PH.

\subsection{Thick cylinder}
\label{sec:num-cylinder}

As a non-polynomial benchmark with curved boundaries we use the thick cylinder under
internal pressure of \citet[Sec.~5.9]{Rajendran2010technique} (quarter annulus
$1\le r\le3$, $E=1000$, $\nu=0.25$, plane stress, Lam\'e solution), computed in the
companion study of the code base (\texttt{studies/thick\_cylinder}) with all three
formulations. The meshes consist of $2N\times2N$ eight-node elements generated with gmsh
\citep{Geuzaine2009Gmsh}, $N=1,2,4,8$, lifted to degree $p$ with exact boundary arcs; in
every $2\times2$ super-element the internal mid-side nodes are rotated by $\alpha$ (mesh
sequence B of \citet{Rajendran2010technique}), so that the relative distortion is the
same on every mesh. For $\alpha\le30^\circ$ all elements are valid; for $\alpha\ge45^\circ$
elements fold on the finest mesh ($\det\matr J<0$ at quadrature points), and the maximum
relative nodal error $e_n$ is used instead of the energy error. \Cref{tab:cylinder} lists
the errors on the finest mesh ($N=8$), the observed rates between $N=4$ and $8$ and the
largest Lebesgue constant of the metric interpolation over the elements;
\cref{fig:cylinder} shows $e_n$ versus $\alpha$.

\paragraph{Valid meshes} Without distortion the three formulations are equivalent
($e_E=2.7\cdot10^{-6}$, $2.7\cdot10^{-9}$, $2.7\cdot10^{-12}$ for $p=4,6,8$). With
distortion, \PP\ loses about half of the convergence order ($r=2.1$--$2.2$, $3.2$--$3.5$,
$4.4$--$4.8$ for $p=4,6,8$ at $\alpha=15^\circ,30^\circ$). \PH\ converges with the optimal
order ($r=3.9$, $5.9$, $7.9$) and keeps $\Lam_T\le95$. \PM\ is the most accurate element at
$\alpha=15^\circ$ for $p\le7$ (by a factor $1.1$--$4.3$ over \PH), consistent with
\cref{sec:num-convergence}, but its rate drops at $p=8$ ($r=5.6$, $\Lam_Q=1.3\cdot10^{4}$),
where \PH\ becomes more accurate ($3.1\cdot10^{-11}$ versus $1.2\cdot10^{-10}$);
at $\alpha=30^\circ$ its convergence is erratic ($r=0.8$ for $p=4$) and at $p=8$ it is less
accurate than \PP\ ($4.8\cdot10^{-8}$ versus $3.0\cdot10^{-8}$), with $\Lam_Q$ up to
$1.2\cdot10^{6}$. At $\alpha=30^\circ$, $p=8$, \PH\ is $390$ times more accurate than \PM\ and
$245$ times more accurate than \PP. The companion study also reports that the \PH\ error is
$0.78$--$1.00$ times its own interpolation error on all valid meshes, i.e.\ the
Petrov--Galerkin solution is quasi-optimal in this example.

\paragraph{Folded meshes} On the folded meshes \PH\ is the most accurate element in most
cases with $3\le p\le6$, often by two orders of magnitude (e.g.\ $\alpha=45^\circ$, $p=6$:
$e_n=2.9\cdot10^{-9}$ versus $2.9\cdot10^{-7}$ for \PP\ and $3.5\cdot10^{-5}$ for \PM;
$\alpha=90^\circ$, $p=6$: $2.0\cdot10^{-7}$ versus $1.1\cdot10^{-4}$ and $3.0\cdot10^{-4}$),
whereas \PM\ is less accurate than \PP\ in almost all folded cases and rejected for
$p\ge6$ at $\alpha=60^\circ$. The limits discussed in \cref{sec:num-convergence} appear here
as well: at $p=2$ ($\alpha=60^\circ,90^\circ$) and at $\alpha=90^\circ$, $p=4$, \PH\ is less
accurate than \PP\ ($e_n=1.1$ at $\alpha=90^\circ$, $p=2$); at $\alpha=45^\circ$, $p=8$, it
stagnates at the level of \PP\ ($2.4\cdot10^{-9}$ versus $4.0\cdot10^{-9}$); and for $p\ge7$ at
$\alpha=60^\circ,90^\circ$ its interpolation matrix is singular to working precision on some
folded elements, so that only \PP\ can be used. On folded meshes, the hybrid space therefore extends the range
of usable degrees compared with $\Qp_p(s,t)$, but it is not a general remedy.
