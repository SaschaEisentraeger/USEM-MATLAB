\section{Numerical studies}
\label{sec:numerics}

All computations use the MATLAB/GNU Octave implementation of the unsymmetric spectral
element (USEM code base) in IEEE double precision. The data were produced with GNU
Octave~8.4 by the scripts in \texttt{manuscript/studies}. If not stated otherwise, GLL
nodes, $p_\xi=p_\eta=p$, an isotropic material with $\nu=0.3$ (plane stress) and
$2p\times2p$ Gauss points for $\matr K_e$ and the consistent mass matrix are used. The
geometry is generated from 8-node (serendipity) input elements and lifted to degree
$p$, and therefore the element maps are at most biquadratic ($g\le2$). In the following,
three formulations are compared: \PP\ (classical spectral element), \PM\ (metric trial
space $\Qp_p(s,t)$, Lagrange basis, \cref{sec:basis}) and \PH\ (hybrid trial space
$\Tp_p$, \cref{sec:hybrid}).

\subsection{Test elements and node geometry}
\label{sec:num-elements}

Four single elements are used: a parallelogram (affine), a trapezoid (straight
edges, bilinear map), an element with two curved edges (``curved'') and an element
with angular and curved-edge distortion (``general''). Their 8-node coordinates are
listed in \cref{app:elements}. \Cref{fig:nodes} shows the GLL nodes of the general
element for $p=8$ in the three coordinate systems involved. In the scaled skew
coordinates, in which $\Qp_p(s,t)$ interpolates, the nodes form a visibly perturbed
tensor grid. In the reference coordinates, on the other hand, in which the complement
$\Cp_p$ of the hybrid space interpolates, they form the exact GLL grid.

\subsection{Conditioning of the Vandermonde matrix and accuracy of the shape functions}
\label{sec:num-conditioning}

\Cref{fig:kappa} shows $\cond_2(\matr V)$ for the four bases of $\Qp_p(s,t)$ and for
the hybrid space. \Cref{fig:kron} depicts the error of the Kronecker property of the
computed shape functions, $e_K=\max_{l,k}|M_l^e(\bx_k)-\delta_{lk}|$, which is the
error for unit nodal data (\cref{rem:reproduction}).

\paragraph{Affine element} With monomials, $\cond_2(\matr V)$ grows from
$2.9\cdot10^{2}$ ($p=4$) over $2.9\cdot10^{5}$ ($p=8$) to $3.5\cdot10^{11}$ ($p=16$),
i.e.\ by a factor of about $5.8\approx(1+\sqrt2)^2$ per degree, the square of the 1D
growth. Legendre polynomials give $\cond_2=10.2$, $24.4$, $55.3$, Chebyshev
polynomials $\cond_2\le3$, and the Lagrange basis yields $\cond_2=1$ for every $p$, as
expected from \cref{cor:affine}. Accordingly, $e_K$ grows with $p$ as $\cond_2(\matr V)u$ for
the monomials ($2.0\cdot10^{-12}$ at $p=8$, $1.1\cdot10^{-6}$ at $p=16$) and is at most
$1.1\cdot10^{-28}$ for the Lagrange basis. The asymmetry
$\|\matr K_e-\matr K_e^\T\|_F/\|\matr K_e\|_F$, zero in exact arithmetic, follows
$e_K$:

The identity of \cref{prop:kappa} is reproduced to $4.1\cdot10^{-14}$ (relative) for
all $p\le16$ on this element, and to within the expected round-off ($\le1.8\cdot10^{-11}$)
on the distorted elements.

\paragraph{Distorted elements} On the trapezoid, the curved and the general element,
the three bases of $[-1,1]$ (with bounding-box scaling) lie within a factor of
about~3 of one another. Compared with the monomials, they improve the condition number by
a factor of 4--13 at $p=4$ and of $4.6\cdot10^{3}$--$4.5\cdot10^{4}$ at $p=16$ (general element: $\cond_2=1.7\cdot10^{6}$ versus $4.7$--$8.4\cdot10^{4}$ at
$p=8$, $9.0\cdot10^{14}$ versus $0.9$--$2.0\cdot10^{11}$ at $p=16$). With monomials,
the trapezoid at $p=16$ is rejected as singular to working precision. In the Lagrange
basis, $e_K$ is reduced accordingly (trapezoid, curved and general element at
$p=16$: $7.1\cdot10^{-6}$, $6.0\cdot10^{-9}$ and $1.8\cdot10^{-6}$, versus rejection,
$4.9\cdot10^{-5}$ and $6.5\cdot10^{-4}$ with monomials). The remaining growth is caused by
the extrapolation of the metric interpolant to the bounding box (\cref{prop:kappa}). On
the general element, for instance, the
factor $\cond_1(\matr V)/\|\matr V\|_1=\max_d\lambda_{\mathcal U}(\hat{\vect s}_d)$ is
$9.8\cdot10^{4}$ at $p=8$, where $\Lam_Q=3.2\cdot10^{2}$, and
$1.4\cdot10^{11}$ at $p=16$, where $\Lam_Q=6.9\cdot10^{6}$. The hybrid space has a different
interpolation problem. Its Vandermonde matrix is as well conditioned as that of
$\Qp_p(s,t)$ for $p\le3$, and from $p=4$ on it is better conditioned than that of
$\Qp_p(s,t)$ in any basis, namely by a factor
of $2$--$6$ at $p=4$ and of $1.6\cdot10^{3}$--$1.9\cdot10^{5}$ at $p=16$. For instance, we
obtain $\cond_2=7.8\cdot10^{2}$ versus $\ge1.3\cdot10^{5}$ (trapezoid, $p=8$) and
$1.7$--$2.0\cdot10^{6}$ versus $\ge8.5\cdot10^{10}$ (trapezoid and general element,
$p=16$). The error of the Kronecker property satisfies $e_K\le3.2\cdot10^{-11}$ for
all elements and $p\le16$.

\subsection{Lebesgue constants}
\label{sec:num-lebesgue}

The Lebesgue constants are evaluated on a uniform grid of $(6p+11)^2$ reference
points mapped to the element. \Cref{fig:lebesgue} shows them versus $p$ for the three
distorted test elements. In \cref{fig:sweep}, they are depicted versus the amount of
distortion $\delta$ for three one-parameter families on $\hat\Omega$: (i) a ``bent''
element (mid-side nodes of the bottom and top edge moved by $\delta$ in $y$, so that
$x=\xi$, $y=\eta+\delta(1-\xi^2)$ and $\det\matr J\equiv1$), (ii) an element with one
curved edge and (iii) a trapezoid (top corners moved inwards by $\delta$; bilinear).

\paragraph{Dependence on the degree} On all distorted elements, $\Lam_Q$ grows
exponentially with $p$: on the trapezoid from $10.4$ ($p=4$) over $7.7\cdot10^{2}$
($p=8$) to $3.9\cdot10^{7}$ ($p=16$), on the curved element to $5.5\cdot10^{4}$ and on
the general element to $6.9\cdot10^{6}$ at $p=16$. As expected from \cref{cor:defect},
the hybrid space gives $\Lam_T=\Lam_N$ exactly on the trapezoid ($6.09$ at $p=16$). On the
curved element, $\Lam_T\le1.07\,\Lam_N$ holds for $p\ge3$ ($1.14\,\Lam_N$ at $p=2$). On the
general element, $\Lam_T\le1.05\,\Lam_N$ for $3\le p\le10$ ($1.11\,\Lam_N$ at $p=2$). For
higher $p$, the ratio grows to $1.12$ ($p=12$) and $1.40$ ($p=16$, $\Lam_T=8.5$ versus
$\Lam_N=6.1$), which is still almost six orders of magnitude below $\Lam_Q$ at that degree.

\paragraph{Dependence on the distortion} \Cref{fig:sweep} separates the effect of the
distortion from that of the degree. For the bent element, whose Jacobian determinant is
constant, $\Lam_Q$ grows from the GLL value to $8.2$, $1.9\cdot10^{2}$ and
$6.4\cdot10^{3}$ at $\delta=0.5$ for $p=4,8,12$. Consequently, the growth is not caused by
a degenerating Jacobian. In contrast, $\Lam_T$ stays within $11\,\%$ of $\Lam_N$ over the
whole range. For the element with one curved edge, $\Lam_T=\Lam_N$ (to two digits) up to
$\delta\approx0.4$, and $\Lam_T$ grows beyond. At the extreme $\delta=1$ (mid-side node
moved by half the edge length), we obtain $\Lam_T=5.7$, $65$, $8.6\cdot10^{2}$ for
$p=4,8,12$, compared with $\Lam_Q=71$, $4.6\cdot10^{4}$, $3.2\cdot10^{7}$. For the trapezoid, $\Lam_T=\Lam_N$ exactly
for all $\delta$ (\cref{cor:defect}), whereas $\Lam_Q$ reaches $1.4\cdot10^{11}$ at
$\delta=0.855$, $p=12$, and $\Qp_{12}(s,t)$ is rejected as singular to working precision at
$\delta=0.9$. The condition numbers of the Vandermonde matrices behave alike
(trapezoid, $p=12$, $\delta=0.81$: $\cond_2=1.1\cdot10^{12}$ for $\Qp_p(s,t)$ in the
Lagrange basis, $4.8\cdot10^{6}$ for $\Tp_p$).

\subsection{Deviation from the classical element}
\label{sec:num-deviation}

\Cref{prop:representation} writes both metric families as the parametric shape functions
plus a correction. \Cref{tab:deviation} quantifies the correction at the $(2p)^2$ Gauss
points of $\matr K_e$, $d_N=\max_{k,q}|M_k^e(\bx_q)-N_k(\bx_q)|$, and its effect on the
stiffness matrix, $d_K=\|\matr K_e-\matr K_e^{\PP}\|_F/\|\matr K_e^{\PP}\|_F$. For
$\Qp_p(s,t)$, the correction grows exponentially with $p$, to $d_N=6.8\cdot10^{3}$ on the
trapezoid and $3.9\cdot10^{3}$ on the general element at $p=12$. In other words, the
metric shape functions deviate from the parametric ones by up to $10^{3}$--$10^{4}$
between the nodes, and $\|\matr K_e-\matr K_e^{\PP}\|_F$ reaches $9$ (curved), $3.4\cdot10^{2}$
(general) and $7.4\cdot10^{2}$ (trapezoid) times $\|\matr K_e^{\PP}\|_F$. For $\Tp_p$, on the
other hand, the correction vanishes to round-off on the trapezoid (\cref{cor:defect}) and
stays bounded on the curved elements ($d_N\le6.4\cdot10^{-2}$ and $d_K\le4.2\cdot10^{-2}$
for all $p\le12$). On the curved element, it even decreases with $p$.
Thus, \PH\ is a small, $p$-robust perturbation of the classical element, confined to the
completeness defect. This explains its classical-like spectral behaviour
(\cref{sec:num-spectra}).

\paragraph{Bounding-box scaling} The Lagrange basis is always used together with the
scaling \eqref{eq:bbox}. In \cref{tab:scaling}, the two effects are separated. The scaling
alone does not help the monomials (their condition number changes by less than a factor
$2$, in either direction), and therefore the gain over the original construction comes
from the basis. On elements whose nodes leave $[-1,1]^2$ in skew coordinates (trapezoid,
general element), however, the Lagrange basis retains this gain at high degree only with
the scaling. Without it, we obtain $\cond_2=2.2\cdot10^{15}$ instead of $8.6\cdot10^{11}$ on
the trapezoid at $p=16$, which is no better than with monomials. On the curved element,
whose nodes lie in $[-1,1]^2$, the scaling has no effect, and on the parallelogram it is
the identity. Note that for $p\le4$ the scaling can slightly increase the condition number.

\subsection{Patch tests}
\label{sec:num-patch}

\paragraph{Distorted meshes} Three $3\times2$ meshes on $[0,3]\times[0,2]$ are
considered: two with curved-edge distortion $0.15$ and $0.20$ and one with combined
angular and curved-edge distortion (\cref{app:elements}). The minimum of $\det\matr J$ over
the elements is $0.16$, $0.09$ and $0.014$, respectively, relative to $0.25$ for the
undistorted mesh. The meshes are loaded by prescribing the harmonic field
$\vect u=(\operatorname{Re}z^p,-\operatorname{Im}z^p)$, $z=(x-1.3)+\mathrm i(y-0.7)$, at all
boundary nodes. This is an equilibrium field of total degree $p$ without body force.
Therefore, both metric spaces are expected to reproduce it exactly, whereas \PP\ is
expected to fail on the curved elements (\cref{sec:formulation}).
\Cref{tab:patch} lists the maximum relative nodal error. Both metric spaces pass for every
$p$, and \PP\ fails. The round-off level of \PM\ grows with $p$ to
$1.2\cdot10^{-13}$--$8.1\cdot10^{-13}$ at $p=10$, whereas \PH\ stays at
$\le3.1\cdot10^{-15}$ (cf.\ \cref{rem:reproduction}). For the smooth field
$\vect u=(e^x\cos y,-e^x\sin y)$ on the same meshes, all three elements converge
exponentially in $p$. For $p\le8$, \PM\ is in most cases the more accurate unsymmetric
element (by factors up to $17$), while at $p=9,10$ \PH\ is more accurate or within a factor
$1.5$. Compared with \PP, \PH\ is $3.6$--$150$ times more accurate for $p=5,\dots,10$
(curved $0.20$).

\paragraph{Folded elements} The nodal rotation patch test of
\citet[Secs.~5.5--5.7]{Rajendran2010technique} uses the panel $[0,2]\times[-1,1]$
discretised by $2\times2$ quadratic elements whose internal mid-side nodes are rotated by the angle
$\theta$ about the centre node (meshes a--f of that paper). For $\theta\ge90^\circ$, all four elements fold
($\det\matr J\le0$ in parts of each element). The elements are lifted to degree $p$ with
the interior nodes taken from the bilinear corner map. The load cases tension, pure bending
and end shear have exact solutions of degree $1$, $2$ and $3$, and therefore all of them
lie in the trial spaces for $p\ge3$. \Cref{tab:panel} shows the maximum nodal error over
the three load cases, and the meshes are depicted in \cref{fig:meshes}. Both metric spaces
pass for all $\theta$. At $p=8$, the round-off level of \PM\ rises to $10^{-5}$
($\theta=135^\circ$, $225^\circ$), whereas that of \PH\ is one to two orders of magnitude
lower. The classical element has nodal errors of $13$--$140\,\%$ for the shear load
on the folded meshes ($p=3,\dots,8$).

\subsection{Convergence on meshes with persistent distortion}
\label{sec:num-convergence}

The unit square is discretised by $N\times N$ elements, $N=2,4,8,16$, whose interior
mid-side nodes are moved normal to their edge by $\delta h$ (\cref{app:elements}).
Thus, the element shapes are the same on every mesh, and the distortion does not vanish
under refinement, which is the worst case for the classical element. For $\delta=0.1$ and
$0.2$, all elements are valid (minimum $\det\matr J$ at the quadrature points $0.20$ and
$0.095$, relative to $0.25$ for the undistorted mesh). For $\delta=0.3$, the elements fold
near two corners ($\det\matr J<0$ at quadrature points). The exact solution
$\vect u=(\operatorname{Re}f,-\operatorname{Im}f)$, $f(z)=e^{2z}$, $z=x+\mathrm iy$, is an
equilibrium field without body force and is prescribed at all boundary nodes. The relative
energy-norm error $e_E$ is evaluated with the strains of the trial functions of each
formulation and $(2p+2)^2$ Gauss points. On the folded meshes, the energy integral
contains $1/\det\matr J$ and is not meaningful. Therefore, the maximum relative nodal error
$e_n$ is used instead. The errors are depicted in \cref{fig:convergence}, and the observed
rates are compiled in \cref{tab:rates}.

\paragraph{Classical element} Due to the persistent distortion, \PP\ is expected to
converge with reduced rates. We observe rates far below $p$, of the order of
$p/2$ ($r_{8,16}=0.89$, $0.82$, $1.84$, $1.72$, $2.80$ for $p=2,\dots,6$ at
$\delta=0.2$), which is consistent with
$\Pp_{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(x,y)\subset\Qp_p(\xi,\eta)\circ\vect F_e^{-1}$ for biquadratic maps
(\cref{sec:formulation}). On the finest mesh, it is one to four and a half orders of
magnitude less accurate than the stable metric elements.

\paragraph{Moderate distortion ($\delta=0.1$)} Both metric spaces converge with the optimal
rate $p$ for all $p\le6$. \PM\ is, however, the more accurate one, and the ratio
$e_E^{\PH}/e_E^{\PM}$ on the finest mesh grows from $1.3$ ($p=2$) to $5.5$ ($p=6$). This is
the expected price of the hybrid space for smooth solutions. Here, $\Qp_p(s,t)$ contains
Cartesian polynomials up to total degree $2p$, whereas $\Tp_p$ contains them only up to
$p$, and therefore the constant of the best approximation is smaller for $\Qp_p(s,t)$.

\paragraph{Strong distortion ($\delta=0.2$)} For $p\le4$, the picture is the same (ratio
$2$--$5$ in favour of \PM, optimal rates for both). For $p=5$ and $6$, however, \PM\
\emph{diverges} under refinement. For $p=5$, its error grows from $5.5\cdot10^{-7}$ ($N=4$)
to $5.8\cdot10^{-3}$ ($N=16$), and on the finest mesh it is even larger than that of \PP.
In contrast, \PH\ converges with $r_{8,16}=4.97$ and $5.98$ and reaches $3.1\cdot10^{-9}$ and
$2.9\cdot10^{-11}$, i.e.\ six and seven orders of magnitude below \PM. Since the elements
are the same on all meshes, the element-level quantities (Lebesgue constants, condition
numbers) do not change with $N$, and by \cref{lem:interp} the interpolation error in
$\Qp_p(s,t)$ decreases with the optimal order. Therefore, the divergence of \PM\ cannot be
attributed to approximation. Instead, it indicates a loss of stability of the assembled
Petrov--Galerkin operator as the number of elements grows. This is consistent with the spurious eigenvalues of
\cref{sec:num-spectra} and with the observations for the thick cylinder in
\cref{sec:num-cylinder}.

\paragraph{Folded elements ($\delta=0.3$)} \PM\ diverges for $p\ge4$ (nodal error up to
$44$ at $p=5$, $N=16$). \PH\ converges in the nodal error (e.g.\ $p=5$:
$8.1\cdot10^{-5}$, $1.7\cdot10^{-6}$, $3.0\cdot10^{-8}$, $4.9\cdot10^{-10}$ for
$N=2,\dots,16$), but less regularly than on the valid meshes ($p=6$ stagnates at
$4\cdot10^{-10}$ on the two finest meshes). Moreover, the energy-norm error stagnates at
$10^{-8}$ for $p\ge5$. This is due to the fact that the integrand $\propto1/\det\matr J$ of
the complement part of $\matr K_e$ (\cref{sec:hybrid-gradients}) is singular where
$\det\matr J$ changes sign and, therefore, is not integrated accurately by Gauss
quadrature. \PP\ converges in the
nodal error with reduced rates ($2.8\cdot10^{-6}$ for $p=5$, $N=16$).

\subsection{Eigenvalues and transient stability}
\label{sec:num-spectra}

\paragraph{Generalised eigenvalues} For the same three meshes with $\vect u=\vect0$ on
the boundary and the consistent mass matrix ($\rho=1$), the complete spectrum of
$\matr K\bm\phi=\lambda\matr M\bm\phi$, $\lambda=\omega^2$, is computed. \Cref{tab:spectrum}
gives the number of eigenvalues with $\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$ and
$\max|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|/|\lambda|$. \Cref{fig:spectrum} depicts the maximum relative
error of the real parts of the 8 eigenvalues of smallest modulus. Here, the classical
element on the undistorted mesh with $p=12$ serves as reference. \PM\ produces eigenvalues with
$\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$ from $p=5$ (curved $0.20$, angular + curved $0.30$) or $p=7$
(curved $0.15$) on, up to 25 of them at $p=10$, with
$|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|/|\lambda|\to1$. These eigenvalues enter the low end of the
spectrum, and the error of the lowest eigenvalues jumps to $1.9$ (curved $0.20$,
$p=6$) and $0.35$ (angular + curved $0.30$, $p=7$) and stagnates at $10^{-5}$--$10^{-3}$ for
higher $p$. The error of \PP, on the other hand, decreases to a plateau of
$4.4$--$4.5\cdot10^{-7}$ for $p\ge8$, which is common to all meshes and formulations
without spurious modes. This plateau reflects the accuracy of the reference values
(classical element, $p=12$) and the corner singularities of the clamped rectangle. Note
that differences below this level are not significant. \PH, in contrast, has no
eigenvalue with negative real part on any mesh and for any $p$, and
$|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|/|\lambda|\le1.7\cdot10^{-2}$ holds over the whole spectrum. The
error of its lowest eigenvalues is between $0.32$ and $1.07$ times that of \PP\ for every
mesh and $p$ (\cref{fig:spectrum}), whereas the error of \PM\ is up to $4\cdot10^{4}$ times
larger.

\paragraph{Growth rates and transient response} The relevant stability measure for
$\matr M\ddot{\vect u}+\matr K\vect u=\vect 0$ is not the sign of
$\operatorname{Re}\lambda$ alone: every eigenvalue that is not real and positive yields
solutions $e^{\pm\mathrm i\sqrt\lambda t}\bm\phi$, one of which grows with the rate
$|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt\lambda|$. \Cref{tab:growth} lists the largest growth exponent per
fundamental period $T_1=2\pi/\omega_1$ (of \PP),
$\mu=T_1\max|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt\lambda|$, and the corresponding exponent of the
trapezoidal rule (Newmark, $\beta=1/4$, $\gamma=1/2$, $\Delta t=T_1/20$),
$\mu_\Delta=20\ln\max|z|$ with the amplification factors
$z=(1+\mathrm ic)/(1-\mathrm ic)$, $c=\pm\sqrt\lambda\,\Delta t/2$. \PP\ is stable
($\mu=0$). For \PM, $\mu$ grows from $\mathcal O(1)$ at $p=2$ to $10^{2}$--$10^{4}$ at
$p=6,7$, and $\mu_\Delta$ reaches $5$--$33$ per period. Thus, a perturbation at round-off
level is amplified to $\mathcal O(1)$ within a few periods. For \PH, $\mu\le0.65$ and
$\mu_\Delta\le0.16$ on all meshes and for all $p$ ($\le0.11$ for $p\ge3$). Therefore, we
have to admit that \PH\ is \emph{not} free of growing modes. Their growth is, however,
slower than that of \PM\ by a factor of $2$--$3$ at $p=2$, $10$--$30$ at $p=3$ and up to
four orders of magnitude ($\mu$) at $p=7$, and $\mu_\Delta$ does not increase with $p$.

\Cref{fig:transient} shows the free vibration from a smooth initial displacement,
$\vect u_0=(\sin\frac{\pi x}{3}\sin\frac{\pi y}{2},\ \sin\frac{2\pi x}{3}\sin\frac{\pi y}{2})$,
perturbed by $10^{-10}$ (relative) to emulate round-off, over $50\,T_1$ with the trapezoidal
rule and $\Delta t=T_1/20$. For \PM, the response grows exponentially and exceeds $10^{29}$
(the computation is stopped at $10^{30}$) for every $p=4,\dots,8$ on both meshes, including
$p=4$, where all eigenvalues have positive real part. \PH\ and \PP\ remain bounded
($\max|\vect u(t)|/\max|\vect u_0|\le1.81$ for all $p$), and their responses are
indistinguishable in the plot. Over $50\,T_1$, the residual growth of \PH\ amplifies the
round-off perturbation by at most $e^{50\mu_\Delta}\le e^{4.4}$ ($p=4,\dots,8$), which is
invisible at the scale of the response. For very long integration times, or for time steps
that resolve the growing high-frequency modes, this residual growth of \PH\ would
eventually become visible. In order to remove it, a dissipative integrator or an
energy-consistent formulation \citep{Wang2022Temporal,Zaehringer2026On} would be needed.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:num-cost}

Run times of the reference implementation (GNU Octave~8.4, one core, median of seven
runs, general element) are compiled in \cref{tab:timing}. The Lagrange basis costs
$1.0$--$1.34$ times as much as the monomials. The hybrid shape functions are $2$--$35$
times more expensive to evaluate than those of \PM\ in this implementation, because the Jacobian at the query
points is recomputed from the parametric shape-function derivatives and the basis matrix is
assembled column by column. Both are implementation details rather than inherent
costs, since the element routines already provide $\matr J(\bxi_q)$. At the level of the
element stiffness matrix, which is dominated by the quadrature loop, the overhead of \PH\
is $7$--$46\,\%$ over the classical element and $18$--$33\,\%$ over \PM\ ($p=2,\dots,12$).
The cost of solving the unsymmetric global system (LU instead of Cholesky) is the same for
\PM\ and \PH.

\subsection{Thick cylinder}
\label{sec:num-cylinder}

\paragraph{Set-up} As a last example, we use the thick cylinder under internal pressure of
\citet[Sec.~5.9]{Rajendran2010technique} as a benchmark with curved boundaries and
non-polynomial solutions (quarter annulus $1\le r\le3$, $E=1000$, $\nu=0.25$, plane
stress, internal pressure $1000$). It is computed with all three formulations in the
companion study of the code base (\texttt{studies/thick\_cylinder}, $1512$ solutions).
The meshes consist of $2N\times2N$ 8-node elements, $N=1,2,4,8$, generated with gmsh
\citep{Geuzaine2009Gmsh}. They are lifted to degree $p=2,\dots,8$ with the arcs
represented exactly (Gordon--Hall blending \citep{Gordon1973Transfinite}). In every
$2\times2$ super-element, the internal mid-side nodes are rotated by
$\alpha\in\{0^\circ,15^\circ,30^\circ,45^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ\}$ (mesh sequence B of
\citet{Rajendran2010technique}, \cref{fig:meshes}), so that the relative distortion
is the same on every mesh and the element maps are not asymptotically affine.
Elements fold for $\alpha=45^\circ$ ($N\ge2$) and $\alpha\ge60^\circ$. Three fields are
computed, namely the Lam\'e solution, a manufactured polynomial of total degree~5 and the
analytic field $u_x=\tfrac1{10}(\sin3X\cos2Y+e^{XY})$, $u_y=\tfrac1{10}(\cos2X\sin3Y+Ye^{-X})$,
$X=x/3$, $Y=y/3$. The body forces and tractions are derived from the exact fields. The
errors are measured as the relative energy-norm error $\eE$ (strains of the respective trial
functions) and as the maximum relative nodal error $e_n$. Note that on folded meshes only
$e_n$ is meaningful. Cases in which the code
base finds the interpolation matrix singular to working precision
($\mathrm{rcond}<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$) are rejected.

\paragraph{Convergence on valid meshes} The energy error of the analytic and of the
Lam\'e field at $\alpha=30^\circ$ is depicted in \cref{fig:cyl-h}. The observed rates
between $N=4$ and $N=8$ are compiled in \cref{tab:cylrates}, and the errors on the finest
mesh for all angles in \cref{tab:cylinder}. Without distortion, the three formulations are
equivalent ($\eE=2.7\cdot10^{-6}$,
$2.7\cdot10^{-9}$, $2.7\cdot10^{-12}$ for the Lam\'e field and $p=4,6,8$). With distortion, \PP\
converges with about half the optimal order, as expected from
$\Pp_{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(x,y)\subset\Qp_p(\xi,\eta)\circ\vect F_e^{-1}$. \PH\ converges with
the optimal order $p$ for all $p$ at $15^\circ$ and $30^\circ$ (Lam\'e: $1.96$--$7.87$; analytic:
$2.00$--$6.02$) and keeps $\Lam_T\le95$ (\cref{tab:cyllebesgue}). \PM\ is the most accurate
element at $\alpha=15^\circ$ for $p\le7$ (by a factor $1.1$--$4.3$ over \PH\ for the Lam\'e field),
which is consistent with \cref{sec:num-convergence}. At $p=8$, however, its rate drops
($\Lam_Q=1.3\cdot10^{4}$), and \PH\ becomes more accurate ($3.1\cdot10^{-11}$ versus
$1.2\cdot10^{-10}$). At $\alpha=30^\circ$, \PM\ is optimal only for $p\le3$. For higher degrees,
its error is erratic and can even increase under refinement (analytic field, $p=4$:
$\eE=2.2\cdot10^{-4}$ at $N=4$, $4.3\cdot10^{-4}$ at $N=8$). At $p=8$ (Lam\'e), it is less
accurate than \PP\ ($4.8\cdot10^{-8}$ versus $3.0\cdot10^{-8}$), and on these meshes $\Lam_Q$
reaches $4.2\cdot10^{6}$ ($p=7$). At $\alpha=30^\circ$, $p=8$, \PH\ is
$390$ times more accurate than \PM\ and $245$ times more accurate than \PP\ for the Lam\'e
field. For the analytic field, its energy error at $N=8$ is $8$--$2.6\cdot10^4$ times smaller
than that of \PP\ ($p=2,\dots,8$).

\paragraph{Approximation versus stability} In order to separate stability from
approximation, the discretisation error is compared with the interpolation error of each
space (\cref{tab:quasiopt}). To this end, the exact nodal values are inserted into the
trial functions. For \PH, the ratio lies between $0.78$ and $1.00$ in all $158$ cases on
the meshes without folds with errors above round-off level, and for \PP\ between $0.84$ and
$1.00$ ($165$ cases). Thus, both are quasi-optimal with a constant close to one in this
example. For \PM, the ratio ranges from $0.07$ to $10^5$. At $\alpha=30^\circ$, $p\ge3$, the
discrete \PM\ solution is up to $64$ times worse \emph{or} up to five times better than its
interpolant, i.e.\ it does not inherit the approximation property of its space. This is
consistent with the loss of stability observed in \cref{sec:num-convergence}.

\paragraph{Lebesgue constants, conditioning and spectra} \Cref{tab:cyllebesgue} lists the
maxima over the elements of the finest mesh. The Lebesgue constant of \PM\ reaches $10^3$ at
$30^\circ$, $p=4$, and $1.3\cdot10^8$ at $45^\circ$, $p=8$. In contrast, that of \PH\ stays
within $30\,\%$ of the GLL value up to $15^\circ$ and $\le95$ at $30^\circ$. The condition
number $\cond_2(\matr V)$ of \PM\ exceeds its Lebesgue constant by up to two orders of
magnitude at $30^\circ$ and almost four at $45^\circ$ (extrapolation to the bounding box,
\cref{prop:kappa}). The condition number of \PH\ is $10^2$--$10^5$ times smaller than that
of \PM\ at $30^\circ$, $p\ge6$. In the companion study, the global stiffness matrices were
analysed as well. For the Lam\'e problem ($14$ cases with $\alpha\le30^\circ$), their smallest
singular values agree to $1\,\%$ for the three elements, but the largest singular value of
\PM\ exceeds that of \PP\ by up to a factor $344$, whereas that of \PH\ stays within a
factor $1.2$. For the analytic field, the error of the discrete solution was expanded in
the singular triplets. In four of the five \PM\ cases tested with $\alpha\ge30^\circ$, a
single one of the six lowest triplets carries $21$--$68\,\%$ of the error. For \PH\ and
\PP, on the other hand, the error is spread over the spectrum.

\paragraph{Folded meshes and unisolvence} \Cref{fig:cylinder} shows $e_n$ on the finest mesh
versus $\alpha$. On the folded meshes, \PH\ is the most accurate element in most cases with
$3\le p\le6$, often by two orders of magnitude (e.g.\ $\alpha=45^\circ$, $p=6$:
$e_n=2.9\cdot10^{-9}$ versus $2.9\cdot10^{-7}$ for \PP\ and $3.5\cdot10^{-5}$ for \PM;
$\alpha=90^\circ$, $p=6$: $2.0\cdot10^{-7}$ versus $1.1\cdot10^{-4}$ and $3.0\cdot10^{-4}$). \PM,
on the other hand, is less accurate than \PP\ in almost all folded cases and is rejected
for $p\ge6$ at $\alpha=60^\circ$. The limits discussed in \cref{sec:num-convergence} appear
here as well. First, at $p=2$ ($\alpha=60^\circ,90^\circ$) and at $\alpha=90^\circ$, $p=4$, \PH\
is less accurate than \PP\ (errors of order $10^{-2}$--$1$, non-monotone in $N$, as for the
other two elements). Second, at $\alpha=45^\circ$, $p=8$, it stagnates at the level of \PP\
($2.4\cdot10^{-9}$ versus $4.0\cdot10^{-9}$). Finally, for $p\ge7$ at $\alpha=60^\circ,90^\circ$,
its interpolation matrix is singular to working precision on some folded elements, so that
only \PP\ can be used. The results in \cref{tab:unisolvence} confirm \cref{prop:factor}.
Here, $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr S)$ follows $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr V_T)$ within a factor $30$, and
the singular cases are exactly those with $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr S)\le4\cdot10^{-16}$ (all
other cases: $\ge2\cdot10^{-15}$). Therefore, the hybrid space extends the range of usable
degrees on folded meshes compared with $\Qp_p(s,t)$, but it is not a general remedy.
