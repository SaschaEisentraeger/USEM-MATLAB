\section{Numerical studies}
\label{sec:numerics}
%
All computations use the MATLAB/GNU Octave implementation of the unsymmetric spectral element (USEM code base) in IEEE double precision and the scripts in \texttt{manuscript/studies}. The element-level studies (\cref{sec:num-conditioning,sec:num-lebesgue,sec:num-deviation,sec:num-cost}) were computed with GNU Octave~8.4, and the mesh-level studies (\cref{sec:num-patch,sec:num-convergence,sec:num-spectra,sec:num-cylinder}) with MATLAB R2026a and the calibrated Gauss rules of \cref{sec:num-quadrature}. If not stated otherwise, GLL nodes, $p_\xi=p_\eta=p$ and an isotropic material with Poisson's ratio $\nu=0.3$ in plane stress are used. The geometry is generated from 8-node (Serendipity) input elements and lifted to degree $p$, so that the element maps are at most biquadratic ($q\le2$). Three formulations are compared: \PP\ (conventional spectral element), \PM\ (metric trial space $\Qsk$ in the Lagrange basis of \cref{sec:basis}) and \PH\ (hybrid trial space $\Tp_p$ of \cref{sec:hybrid}). By \cref{prop:invariance}, the trial functions of \PM\ are those of the companion paper, which uses monomials, up to round-off.

%
\subsection{Quadrature}
\label{sec:num-quadrature}
%
The stiffness integrands of the three formulations are not polynomials of low degree. Those of \PP\ and of the complement of \PH\ contain $1/\det(\matr J)$ (\cref{sec:hybrid-gradients}), and those of \PM\ are polynomials of a high degree in the reference coordinates. In order to compare the formulations and not their quadrature errors, the number of Gauss points per direction, $\nG$, is chosen separately for every formulation, degree and mesh such that the element matrices are integrated to round-off, as in the companion paper \citep{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}. To this end, the element matrices are computed with $\nG=p+1,p+2,\dots$ points and compared with a reference computed with $\max(64,8p)$ points. Here, the reference rule is larger than the rule $\max(40,8p)$ of the companion paper, since 40 points are not sufficient on the nearly degenerate elements of the $3\times2$ mesh with angular and curved-edge distortion. The rule used in the studies is the smallest $\nG$ for which this and the next rule agree with the reference to a relative deviation below $10^{-12}$ in the Frobenius norm, maximized over the elements of the mesh. For the dynamic studies, the consistent mass matrix is included. For \PM\ of high degree, the element matrices themselves carry round-off errors above $10^{-12}$, of the order of $\cond(\matr V)\,u$ (\cref{sec:basis-invariance}). In these cases, the tolerance is raised to ten times the deviation between two reference rules, which amounts to at most $2.2\cdot10^{-11}$ ($p=10$, angular and curved-edge distortion). The calibration uses all elements of the $3\times2$ meshes, all elements of the meshes with $N=2,4,8$ of \cref{sec:num-convergence} (from $N=4$ on, the element shapes repeat exactly), and, for the thick cylinder, all elements for $N\le2$ and the elements at the two arcs and in the ring closest to $r=2$ for $N=4,8$. The loads are integrated with $\max(3p+2,\nG)$ and the errors with $\nG+4$ points per direction.

We observe that \PM\ requires $2p+1$ to $2p+3$ points on the meshes with the 8-node geometry, which is consistent with its polynomial integrand, and up to $20$ points for $p=8$ on the thick cylinder, whose elements at the arcs have a geometry of degree $p$. \PP\ and \PH\ require considerably more points, namely $11$--$25$ on the square meshes and up to $51$ on the $3\times2$ mesh with angular and curved-edge distortion, whose smallest Jacobian determinant is $0.014$. The former default rule of the code base, $2p\times2p$ points, therefore gave element matrices with relative errors of up to $5.4\cdot10^{-2}$ on that mesh, $1.1\cdot10^{-2}$ on the square mesh with $\delta=0.2$ and $3.8\cdot10^{-2}$ on the thick cylinder with $\alpha=30^\circ$. Note that the patch tests are not affected: for a polynomial solution of degree $p$ on the 8-node geometry, the integrand $\bar{\matr B}_{\mathrm N}^\T\matr C\,\bm\varepsilon(\vect u)$ of the unsymmetric elements is a polynomial of degree at most $3p-1$ in each reference coordinate, since the entries of $\bar{\matr B}_{\mathrm N}$ are of degree $p+1$ and those of $\bm\varepsilon(\vect u)$ of degree $2(p-1)$. Thus, every rule with at least $3p/2$ points satisfies condition (C3) for them. The single-element quantity $d_K$ of \cref{tab:deviation} is unchanged to the digits given when the calibrated rules are used.
% data: data/quadrature/deviation-check.dat (studies/ms_deviation_check.m)

On folded elements, i.e., on the square meshes with $\delta=0.3$ and on the thick-cylinder meshes with $\alpha\ge45^\circ$, no rule converges for \PP\ and \PH. This is not a matter of the number of points. The integrands of these two formulations contain $1/\det(\matr J)$, and $\det(\matr J)$ changes sign along a curve inside a folded element. This singularity is not integrable. Consequently, the stiffness matrices of \PP\ and \PH\ are not defined on folded elements, and the matrices computed by Gauss quadrature depend on the rule: with $2p$ points, they deviate from those with the reference rule by more than $100\,\%$. Only the stiffness matrix of \PM, whose integrand contains $\adj(\matr J)$ and no $\det(\matr J)$ \citep{Rajendran2010technique}, exists on folded elements, and its rule converges. Results of \PP\ and \PH\ on folded meshes are therefore computed with the reference rule and reported only for completeness. They do not measure the accuracy of these elements.
\subsection{Test elements and node geometry}
\label{sec:num-elements}
%
Four single elements are used: a parallelogram (affine), a trapezoid (straight edges, bilinear map), an element with two curved edges (\emph{curved}) and an element with angular and curved-edge distortion (\emph{general}). Their 8-node coordinates are listed in \cref{app:elements}. \Cref{fig:nodes} shows the GLL nodes of the general element for $p=8$ in the three coordinate systems involved. In the scaled skew coordinates, in which $\Qsk$ interpolates, the nodes form a visibly perturbed tensor-product grid. In the reference coordinates, in which the complement $\Cp_p$ of the hybrid space interpolates, they form the exact GLL grid.

\subsection{Conditioning of the Vandermonde matrix and accuracy of the shape functions}
\label{sec:num-conditioning}
%
\Cref{fig:kappa} shows $\cond_2(\matr V)$ for the four bases of $\Qsk$ and for the hybrid space. \Cref{fig:kron} depicts the error of the Kronecker property of the computed shape functions, $e_K=\max_{i,j}|M_i^K(\bx_j)-\delta_{ij}|$, which is the error for unit nodal data (\cref{sec:basis-distorted}).

On the parallelogram, we observe that the condition number for monomials grows from $2.9\cdot10^{2}$ ($p=4$) over $2.9\cdot10^{5}$ ($p=8$) to $3.5\cdot10^{11}$ ($p=16$), i.e., by a factor of about $5.8\approx(1+\sqrt2)^2$ per degree, which is the square of the one-dimensional growth. Legendre polynomials give $\cond_2(\matr V)=10.2$, $24.4$ and $55.3$, Chebyshev polynomials $\cond_2(\matr V)\le3$, and the Lagrange basis yields $\cond_2(\matr V)=1$ for every $p$, as expected from \cref{cor:affine}. Accordingly, $e_K$ grows with $p$ as $\cond_2(\matr V)\,u$ for the monomials ($2.0\cdot10^{-12}$ at $p=8$ and $1.1\cdot10^{-6}$ at $p=16$) and is at most $1.1\cdot10^{-28}$ for the Lagrange basis. The relative asymmetry $\|\matr K_K-\matr K_K^\T\|_F/\|\matr K_K\|_F$ of the stiffness matrix, which vanishes in exact arithmetic, follows $e_K$:

The identity \cref{eq:kappa} is reproduced to $4.1\cdot10^{-14}$ (relative) for all $p\le16$ on this element and to within the expected round-off ($\le1.8\cdot10^{-11}$) on the distorted elements.

On the trapezoid, the curved and the general element, the three bases of $[-1,1]$ – used with the bounding-box scaling – lie within a factor of about 3 of one another. Compared with the monomials, they improve the condition number by a factor of 4--13 at $p=4$ and of $4.6\cdot10^{3}$--$4.5\cdot10^{4}$ at $p=16$. On the general element, e.g., $\cond_2(\matr V)=1.7\cdot10^{6}$ for monomials versus $4.7$--$8.4\cdot10^{4}$ at $p=8$ and $9.0\cdot10^{14}$ versus $0.9$--$2.0\cdot10^{11}$ at $p=16$. With monomials, the trapezoid at $p=16$ is rejected as singular to working precision. In the Lagrange basis, $e_K$ is reduced accordingly: $7.1\cdot10^{-6}$, $6.0\cdot10^{-9}$ and $1.8\cdot10^{-6}$ on the trapezoid, the curved and the general element at $p=16$, compared with rejection, $4.9\cdot10^{-5}$ and $6.5\cdot10^{-4}$ with monomials. The remaining growth is caused by the extrapolation of the metric interpolant to the bounding box (\cref{prop:kappa}). On the general element, the factor $\cond_1(\matr V)/\|\matr V\|_1=\max_d\lambda_{\mathcal U}(\bm g_d)$ is $9.8\cdot10^{4}$ at $p=8$, where $\Lam_Q=3.2\cdot10^{2}$, and $1.4\cdot10^{11}$ at $p=16$, where $\Lam_Q=6.9\cdot10^{6}$.

The hybrid space poses a different interpolation problem. Its Vandermonde matrix is as well conditioned as that of $\Qsk$ for $p\le3$, and from $p=4$ on it is better conditioned than that of $\Qsk$ in any basis, by a factor of $2$--$6$ at $p=4$ and of $1.6\cdot10^{3}$--$1.9\cdot10^{5}$ at $p=16$. For instance, we obtain $\cond_2(\matr V)=7.8\cdot10^{2}$ versus at least $1.3\cdot10^{5}$ on the trapezoid at $p=8$, and $1.7$--$2.0\cdot10^{6}$ versus at least $8.5\cdot10^{10}$ on the trapezoid and the general element at $p=16$. The error of the Kronecker property is $e_K\le3.2\cdot10^{-11}$ for all elements and $p\le16$.

\subsection{Lebesgue constants}
\label{sec:num-lebesgue}
%
The Lebesgue constants are evaluated on a uniform grid of $(6p+11)^2$ reference points mapped to the element. \Cref{fig:lebesgue} shows them versus the degree $p$ for the three distorted test elements. In \cref{fig:sweep}, they are depicted versus a distortion parameter $c$ for three one-parameter families on $\Kh$: (i) a \emph{bent} element, whose mid-side nodes of the bottom and the top edge are moved by $c$ in the $y$-direction, so that $x=\xi$, $y=\eta+c\,(1-\xi^2)$ and $\det(\matr J)\equiv1$, (ii) an element with one curved edge, whose mid-side node of the bottom edge is moved by $c$ in the $y$-direction, and (iii) a trapezoid, whose top corners are moved inwards by $c$ (bilinear map). Since the edges of $\Kh$ have the length 2, the relative sagitta \cref{eq:delta} of the curved edges is $\delta=c/2$.
% code: studies/ms_distortion_sweep.m (familyNodes)

On all distorted elements, $\Lam_Q$ grows exponentially with $p$: on the trapezoid from $10.4$ ($p=4$) over $7.7\cdot10^{2}$ ($p=8$) to $3.9\cdot10^{7}$ ($p=16$), on the curved element to $5.5\cdot10^{4}$ and on the general element to $6.9\cdot10^{6}$ at $p=16$. As expected from \cref{cor:defect}, the hybrid space gives $\Lam_T=\Lam_N$ exactly on the trapezoid ($6.09$ at $p=16$). On the curved element, $\Lam_T\le1.07\,\Lam_N$ holds for $p\ge3$ ($1.14\,\Lam_N$ at $p=2$). On the general element, $\Lam_T\le1.05\,\Lam_N$ for $3\le p\le10$ ($1.11\,\Lam_N$ at $p=2$). For higher degrees, the ratio grows to $1.12$ ($p=12$) and $1.40$ ($p=16$, $\Lam_T=8.5$ versus $\Lam_N=6.1$), which is still almost six orders of magnitude below $\Lam_Q$ at that degree.

\Cref{fig:sweep} separates the effect of the distortion from that of the degree. For the bent element, whose Jacobian determinant is constant, $\Lam_Q$ grows from the GLL value to $8.2$, $1.9\cdot10^{2}$ and $6.4\cdot10^{3}$ at $c=0.5$ for $p=4,8,12$. Consequently, the growth is not caused by a degenerating Jacobian. In contrast, $\Lam_T$ stays within $11\,\%$ of $\Lam_N$ over the whole range. For the element with one curved edge, $\Lam_T=\Lam_N$ (to two digits) up to $c\approx0.4$, and $\Lam_T$ grows beyond. At the extreme $c=1$, i.e., with the mid-side node moved by half the edge length, we obtain $\Lam_T=5.7$, $65$ and $8.6\cdot10^{2}$ for $p=4,8,12$, compared with $\Lam_Q=71$, $4.6\cdot10^{4}$ and $3.2\cdot10^{7}$. For the trapezoid, $\Lam_T=\Lam_N$ exactly for all $c$ (\cref{cor:defect}), whereas $\Lam_Q$ reaches $1.4\cdot10^{11}$ at $c=0.855$ for $p=12$, and $\Qp_{12}(\bar\xi,\bar\eta)$ is rejected as singular to working precision at $c=0.9$. The condition numbers of the Vandermonde matrices behave alike, e.g., $\cond_2(\matr V)=1.1\cdot10^{12}$ for $\Qsk$ in the Lagrange basis and $4.8\cdot10^{6}$ for $\Tp_p$ on the trapezoid with $p=12$ and $c=0.81$.

\subsection{Deviation from the conventional element}
\label{sec:num-deviation}
%
\Cref{prop:representation} writes both metric spaces as the parametric shape functions plus a correction. \Cref{tab:deviation} quantifies this correction at the $(2p)^2$ Gauss points of $\matr K_K$, $d_N=\max_{i,r}|M_i^K(\bx_r)-N_i(\bx_r)|$, and its effect on the stiffness matrix, $d_K=\|\matr K_K-\matr K_K^{\PP}\|_F/\|\matr K_K^{\PP}\|_F$. For $\Qsk$, the correction grows exponentially with $p$, to $d_N=6.8\cdot10^{3}$ on the trapezoid and $3.9\cdot10^{3}$ on the general element at $p=12$. That is to say, the metric shape functions deviate from the parametric ones by up to $10^{3}$--$10^{4}$ between the nodes, and $\|\matr K_K-\matr K_K^{\PP}\|_F$ reaches $9$ (curved), $3.4\cdot10^{2}$ (general) and $7.4\cdot10^{2}$ (trapezoid) times $\|\matr K_K^{\PP}\|_F$. For $\Tp_p$, on the other hand, the correction vanishes to round-off on the trapezoid (\cref{cor:defect}) and stays bounded on the curved elements ($d_N\le6.4\cdot10^{-2}$ and $d_K\le4.2\cdot10^{-2}$ for all $p\le12$). On the curved element, it even decreases with $p$. Thus, \PH\ is a small perturbation of the conventional element, confined to the completeness defect, whose size does not grow with $p$. This explains its spectral behavior, which is close to that of the conventional element (\cref{sec:num-spectra}).

\subsection{Patch tests}
\label{sec:num-patch}
%
Three $3\times2$ meshes on $[0,3]\times[0,2]$ are considered, two with curved interior edges ($\delta=0.15$ and $0.20$) and one with angular and curved-edge distortion (\cref{app:elements}). The smallest value of $\det(\matr J)$ in the elements is $0.16$, $0.09$ and $0.014$, respectively, compared with $0.25$ for the undistorted mesh. The meshes are loaded by prescribing the harmonic field $\vect u=(\operatorname{Re}z^p,-\operatorname{Im}z^p)$ with $z=(x-1.3)+\mathrm i\,(y-0.7)$ at all boundary nodes. This field is an equilibrium solution of total degree $p$ without body force. Therefore, both metric spaces are expected to reproduce it exactly, whereas \PP\ is expected to fail on the curved elements (\cref{sec:completeness}). \Cref{tab:patch} lists the largest relative nodal error. As expected, both metric spaces pass for every $p$, and \PP\ fails. The round-off level of \PM\ grows with $p$ to $1.1\cdot10^{-13}$--$6.9\cdot10^{-13}$ at $p=10$, whereas that of \PH\ stays at or below $2.5\cdot10^{-15}$ (cf.\ \cref{sec:basis-distorted}). For the smooth field $\vect u=(e^x\cos y,-e^x\sin y)$ on the same meshes, all three elements converge exponentially in $p$. For $p\le8$, \PM\ is in most cases the more accurate unsymmetric element, by factors of up to $17$. For $p=9,10$, \PH\ is more accurate in four of the six cases, and \PM\ by at most a factor of $2.1$ in the other two. Compared with \PP, \PH\ is $3.6$--$150$ times more accurate for $p=5,\dots,10$ on the mesh with $\delta=0.20$.

The nodal rotation patch test of \citet[Sects.~5.5--5.7]{Rajendran2010technique} uses the panel $[0,2]\times[-1,1]$, discretized by $2\times2$ quadratic elements whose interior mid-side nodes are rotated by the angle $\theta$ about the center node (meshes a--f of that paper). For $\theta\ge90^\circ$, all four elements are folded, i.e., $\det(\matr J)\le0$ in parts of each element. The elements are lifted to degree $p$ with the interior nodes taken from the bilinear corner map. The load cases tension, pure bending and end shear have exact solutions of degree $1$, $2$ and $3$, so that all of them lie in the trial spaces for $p\ge3$. This study uses $2p\times2p$ Gauss points, which satisfy condition (C3) for these fields as shown in \cref{sec:num-quadrature}. For \PH, this also holds on the folded elements, since the coefficients of the interpolant of such a field on the complement vanish (\cref{sec:hybrid-gradients}), so that the singular part of the integrand does not contribute to the product of the stiffness matrix with the exact nodal displacements. \Cref{tab:panel} shows the largest nodal error over the three load cases, and the meshes are depicted in \cref{fig:meshes}. We observe that both metric spaces pass for all $\theta$, although the exactness argument of \cref{sec:exact} does not cover folded elements. At $p=8$, the round-off level of \PM\ rises to $10^{-5}$ ($\theta=135^\circ$ and $225^\circ$), whereas that of \PH\ is one to two orders of magnitude lower. The conventional element has nodal errors of $13$--$140\,\%$ for the shear load on the folded meshes ($p=3,\dots,8$). Note, however, that its stiffness matrix is not defined on folded elements, so that these values depend on the quadrature rule (\cref{sec:num-quadrature}).

\subsection{Convergence on meshes with persistent distortion}
\label{sec:num-convergence}
%
The unit square is discretized by $N\times N$ elements, $N=2,4,8,16$, whose interior mid-side nodes are moved normal to their edge by $\delta h$, so that the relative sagitta \cref{eq:delta} of every interior edge is $\delta$ (\cref{app:elements}). Thus, the element shapes are the same on every mesh, and the distortion does not vanish under refinement. For the conventional element, this is the worst case (\cref{sec:completeness}). For $\delta=0.1$ and $0.2$, all elements are valid, with the smallest $\det(\matr J)$ at the quadrature points being $0.20$ and $0.095$, compared with $0.25$ for the undistorted mesh. For $\delta=0.3$, the elements fold near two corners, i.e., $\det(\matr J)<0$ at quadrature points. The exact solution $\vect u=(\operatorname{Re}f,-\operatorname{Im}f)$ with $f(z)=e^{2z}$ and $z=x+\mathrm i\,y$ is an equilibrium field without body force and is prescribed at all boundary nodes. The relative energy-norm error $\eE=\enormh{\vect u-\vect u_h}/\enorm{\vect u}$ is evaluated with the strains of the trial functions of each formulation and $(2p+2)^2$ Gauss points. On the folded meshes, the energy integral contains $1/\det(\matr J)$ and is not meaningful, and the largest relative nodal error $e_n$ is used instead. The errors are depicted in \cref{fig:convergence}, and the observed rates are compiled in \cref{tab:rates}.

Due to the persistent distortion, \PP\ is expected to converge with reduced rates. We observe rates far below $p$, of the order of $p/2$ ($r_{8,16}=0.89$, $0.82$, $1.84$, $1.72$ and $2.80$ for $p=2,\dots,6$ at $\delta=0.2$), which is consistent with the complete degree $\lfloor p/2\rfloor$ of the mapped space (\cref{sec:completeness}) and with the rates observed in the companion paper. On the finest mesh, \PP\ is one to four and a half orders of magnitude less accurate than the stable metric elements.

For the moderate distortion $\delta=0.1$, both metric spaces converge with the optimal rate $p$ for all $p\le6$. \PM\ is, however, the more accurate one, and the ratio $e_E^{\PH}/e_E^{\PM}$ on the finest mesh grows from $1.3$ ($p=2$) to $5.6$ ($p=6$). This is the price of the hybrid space for smooth solutions. Here, $\Qsk$ contains Cartesian polynomials up to the total degree $2p$, whereas $\Tp_p$ contains them only up to $p$, so that the best fit in \cref{eq:quasiopt} is smaller for $\Qsk$.

For the strong distortion $\delta=0.2$, the picture is the same for $p\le4$, with ratios of $2$--$5$ in favor of \PM\ and optimal rates for both. For $p=5$ and $6$, however, \PM\ \emph{diverges} under refinement. For $p=5$, its error grows from $5.5\cdot10^{-7}$ ($N=4$) to $5.9\cdot10^{-3}$ ($N=16$), and on the finest mesh it is even larger than that of \PP. In contrast, \PH\ converges with $r_{8,16}=4.97$ and $5.98$ and reaches $3.1\cdot10^{-9}$ and $2.9\cdot10^{-11}$, i.e., about six orders of magnitude less than \PM. Since the elements are the same on all meshes, the element-level quantities, i.e., the Lebesgue constants and the condition numbers, do not change with $N$, and by \cref{lem:interp} the interpolation error in $\Qsk$ decreases with the optimal order. Therefore, the divergence of \PM\ cannot be attributed to the best-fit term of \cref{eq:quasiopt}. Instead, it indicates that the stability factor $\beta_h$ of the assembled unsymmetric system decreases as the number of elements grows. This is consistent with the spurious eigenvalues of \cref{sec:num-spectra} and with the observations for the thick cylinder in \cref{sec:num-cylinder}.

On the folded meshes ($\delta=0.3$), only \PM\ has well-defined stiffness matrices (\cref{sec:num-quadrature}). It converges in the nodal error for $p=2,3$ (e.g., $3.5\cdot10^{-7}$ for $p=3$ and $N=16$), but diverges for $p\ge4$, with nodal errors of up to $45$ at $p=5$ and $N=16$. The results of \PP\ and \PH\ depend on the quadrature rule. With the $2p$ rule, both converged in the nodal error, \PH\ for $p=5$, e.g., to $4.9\cdot10^{-10}$ on the finest mesh. With the reference rule, however, \PH\ diverges for $p=2$, with a nodal error of $25$ at $N=16$, and stagnates at $3.7\cdot10^{-7}$ for $p=5$, and \PP\ no longer converges for $p=3$ and $5$. These computations therefore allow no statement on the accuracy of \PP\ and \PH\ on folded meshes, which is consistent with the fact that their element integrals do not exist there.

\subsection{Eigenvalues and transient stability}
\label{sec:num-spectra}
%
For the three $3\times2$ meshes of \cref{sec:num-patch} with $\vect u=\vect0$ on the boundary and the consistent mass matrix ($\rho=1$), the complete spectrum of $\matr K\bm\phi=\lambda\matr M\bm\phi$ with $\lambda=\omega^2$ is computed. For the conventional element, all eigenvalues are real and positive. \Cref{tab:spectrum} gives the number of eigenvalues with $\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$ and the largest relative imaginary part $\max|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|/|\lambda|$, and \cref{fig:spectrum} depicts the largest relative error of the real parts of the 8 eigenvalues of smallest modulus. Here, the conventional element on the undistorted mesh with $p=12$ serves as reference. We observe that \PM\ produces eigenvalues with $\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$ from $p=5$ (curved $0.20$, angular + curved $0.30$) or $p=7$ (curved $0.15$) on, up to 25 of them at $p=10$, with $|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|/|\lambda|\to1$. These eigenvalues enter the low end of the spectrum. Consequently, the error of the lowest eigenvalues jumps to $1.9$ (curved $0.20$, $p=6$) and $0.35$ (angular + curved $0.30$, $p=7$) and stagnates at $10^{-5}$--$10^{-3}$ for higher degrees. The error of \PP, on the other hand, decreases to $4.4$--$4.5\cdot10^{-7}$ at $p=10$, a value that is common to all meshes and formulations without spurious modes. It reflects the accuracy of the reference values and the corner singularities of the clamped rectangle, so that differences below this level are not significant. \PH, in contrast, has no eigenvalue with negative real part on any mesh and for any $p$, and $|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|/|\lambda|\le1.7\cdot10^{-2}$ holds over the whole spectrum. The error of its lowest eigenvalues is $0.32$ to $1.07$ times that of \PP\ for every mesh and $p$ (\cref{fig:spectrum}), whereas the error of \PM\ is up to $4\cdot10^{4}$ times larger.

For the free vibration $\matr M\ddot{\vect u}+\matr K\vect u=\vect 0$, the sign of $\operatorname{Re}\lambda$ alone is not the relevant stability measure. Every eigenvalue that is not real and positive yields solutions $e^{\pm\mathrm i\sqrt\lambda t}\bm\phi$, one of which grows with the rate $|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt\lambda|$. \Cref{tab:growth} lists the largest growth exponent per fundamental period $T_1=2\pi/\omega_1$ of \PP, $\mu=T_1\max|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt\lambda|$, and the corresponding exponent of the trapezoidal rule (Newmark method with $\beta=1/4$, $\gamma=1/2$ and $\Delta t=T_1/20$), $\mu_\Delta=20\ln\max|z|$, with the amplification factors $z=(1+\mathrm ic)/(1-\mathrm ic)$ and $c=\pm\sqrt\lambda\,\Delta t/2$. \PP\ is stable ($\mu=0$). For \PM, $\mu$ grows from the order of one at $p=2$ to $10^{2}$--$10^{4}$ at $p=6,7$, and $\mu_\Delta$ reaches $5$--$33$ per period. Thus, a perturbation at the level of round-off is amplified to the order of one within a few periods. For \PH, $\mu\le0.65$ and $\mu_\Delta\le0.16$ on all meshes and for all $p$ ($\le0.11$ for $p\ge3$). Therefore, we have to admit that \PH\ is \emph{not} free of growing modes. Their growth is, however, considerably slower than that of \PM. At $p=2$, it is slower by a factor of $2.5$ (angular + curved $0.30$) and $37$ (curved $0.20$), and on the mesh with curved $0.15$ \PH\ shows no growth at all. At $p=3$, the factor amounts to $20$--$30$, and at $p=7$ to up to four orders of magnitude ($\mu$). Moreover, $\mu_\Delta$ does not increase with $p$.

\label{sec:num-cost}

Run times of the reference implementation (GNU Octave~8.4, one core, median of seven runs, general element) are compiled in \cref{tab:timing}. The Lagrange basis costs $1.0$ to $1.34$ times as much as the monomials. The hybrid shape functions are $2$ to $35$ times more expensive to evaluate than those of \PM\ in this implementation, since the Jacobian matrix at the query points is recomputed from the derivatives of the parametric shape functions and the basis matrix is assembled column by column. Both are implementation details rather than inherent costs, since the element routines already provide $\matr J(\bxi_r)$. At the level of the element stiffness matrix, which is dominated by the quadrature loop, the overhead of \PH\ is $7$--$46\,\%$ over the conventional element and $18$--$33\,\%$ over \PM\ ($p=2,\dots,12$). Note, however, that on straight-sided elements \PH\ coincides with \PP\ (\cref{prop:hybrid}), so that the metric functions would have to be computed only on elements with curved edges. This saving is not exploited in the timings of \cref{tab:timing}, which refer to a curved element.

\subsection{Thick cylinder}
\label{sec:num-cylinder}
%
As a last example, we use the thick cylinder under internal pressure of \citet[Sect.~5.9]{Rajendran2010technique} as a benchmark with curved boundaries and non-polynomial solutions: a quarter annulus $1\le r\le3$ with Young's modulus $E=1000$, Poisson's ratio $\nu=0.25$ and the internal pressure $1000$ in plane stress. It is computed with all three formulations by the scripts of the study in \texttt{studies/thick\_cylinder} ($1512$ solutions), here with the calibrated Gauss rules of \cref{sec:num-quadrature}. The meshes consist of $2N\times2N$ 8-node elements, $N=1,2,4,8$, generated with \texttt{Gmsh} \citep{Geuzaine2009Gmsh}. They are lifted to degree $p=2,\dots,8$, with the arcs represented exactly by Gordon-Hall blending \citep{Gordon1973Transfinite}. In every $2\times2$ super-element, the interior mid-side nodes are rotated by $\alpha\in\{0^\circ,15^\circ,30^\circ,45^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ\}$ (mesh sequence B of \citet{Rajendran2010technique}, \cref{fig:meshes}), so that the relative distortion is the same on every mesh and the element maps do not approach affine maps under refinement. Elements fold for $\alpha=45^\circ$ ($N\ge2$) and $\alpha\ge60^\circ$. Three fields are computed, namely the Lam\'e solution, a manufactured polynomial of total degree 5 and the analytic field $u_x=\tfrac1{10}(\sin3X\cos2Y+e^{XY})$, $u_y=\tfrac1{10}(\cos2X\sin3Y+Ye^{-X})$ with $X=x/3$ and $Y=y/3$. The body forces and tractions are derived from the exact fields. The errors are measured by the relative energy-norm error $\eE$, evaluated with the strains of the respective trial functions, and by the largest relative nodal error $e_n$. On folded meshes, only $e_n$ is meaningful. Cases in which the interpolation matrix is singular to working precision ($\mathrm{rcond}<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$) are rejected.

In \cref{fig:cylinder} and \cref{tab:cylinder}, they are therefore given only for completeness. The unisolvence of the hybrid space, on the other hand, does not depend on the quadrature. For $p\ge7$ at $\alpha=60^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, its interpolation matrix is singular to working precision on some folded elements. The results in \cref{tab:unisolvence} confirm \cref{prop:factor}: $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr S)$ follows $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr V_T)$ within a factor of $30$, and the singular cases are exactly those with $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr S)\le4\cdot10^{-16}$, while all other cases have $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr S)\ge2\cdot10^{-15}$. Therefore, the hybrid space is not a remedy for folded elements: its stiffness integrals do not exist there, and at high degree its interpolation problem can become singular.
