\section{Improvement 2: a hybrid metric-parametric trial space}
\label{sec:hybrid}
%
\subsection{Which trial functions are actually needed?}
\label{sec:hybrid-requirements}
%
The trial functions of the formulation of \cref{sec:formulation} have to satisfy three requirements:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] every element has $n=(p+1)^2$ trial functions that interpolate the nodal values, so that the nodal unknowns are shared by neighboring elements and the GLL mass matrix remains diagonal;
  \item[(ii)] they represent the complete polynomials $\Pp_p(x,y)$, so that condition (C1) of \cref{sec:exact} holds for polynomial solutions of degree $p$, which includes the rigid-body modes and the constant strain states;
  \item[(iii)] they do not depend on the position, the orientation and the size of the element.
\end{enumerate}
Requirement (ii) fixes only $(p+1)(p+2)/2$ of the $n$ functions. The remaining $p(p+1)/2$ functions are free. The trial space $\Qsk$ of \PM\ fills them with Cartesian polynomials of total degree $p+1,\dots,2p$, e.g., $\bar\xi^p\bar\eta^p$. On a non-affine element, the nodes do not form a tensor-product grid in $(\bar\xi,\bar\eta)$, and these polynomials of high degree are only weakly determined by the nodal values. We attribute the growth of the Lebesgue constant (\cref{sec:num-lebesgue}) to their interpolation on the perturbed grid (cf.\ \cref{sec:hybrid-representation}). In the reference coordinates, on the other hand, the nodes form the exact GLL tensor grid on every element. This motivates our choice to write only the complete polynomials in the skew coordinates and the remaining functions in the reference coordinates.
%
\subsection{Construction}
\label{sec:hybrid-definition}
%
The hybrid trial space $\Tp_p$ of an element is spanned by two groups of functions:
%
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{L_a(\tilde\xi)\,L_b(\tilde\eta),\quad a+b\le p}_{\text{complete polynomials }\Pp_p(x,y)}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \underbrace{L_a(\xi)\,L_b(\eta),\quad a,b\le p,\quad a+b>p}_{\text{complement }\Cp_p} .
  \label{eq:hybrid}
\end{equation}
%
Here, $L_a$ denotes the Legendre polynomial of degree $a$, $(\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta)$ are the scaled skew coordinates \cref{eq:bbox} and $(\xi,\eta)$ the reference coordinates of the geometry map. Since the scaled skew coordinates are affine functions of $(x,y)$, the first group spans exactly the complete polynomials $\Pp_p(x,y)$. The second group, the complement $\Cp_p$, consists of the $p(p+1)/2$ products of Legendre polynomials whose total degree exceeds $p$, and these are evaluated in the reference coordinates. As for \PM, the shape functions $M_i^K$ of \PH\ are obtained from \cref{eq:vandermonde} with these $n$ functions as basis $\{\phi_c\}$. Since all functions are evaluated at the reference coordinates of the query points, only the geometry map $\bx_K(\xi,\eta)$ is required, but not its inverse.

In contrast to the first group, whose basis can be chosen freely (\cref{sec:basis-invariance}), the choice of the complement is part of the definition of the trial space. On a curved element, the complete polynomials in $(x,y)$ are not polynomials of total degree $p$ in $(\xi,\eta)$, and a different complement, e.g., the monomials $\xi^a\eta^b$ with $a+b>p$, would give different shape functions. The Legendre products in \cref{eq:hybrid} are the natural choice: due to the orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials, every function of $\Cp_p$ is orthogonal on the reference square to all polynomials of total degree $p$ in $(\xi,\eta)$, i.e., $\int_{\Kh}L_a(\xi)L_b(\eta)\,\xi^i\eta^j\dd\xi\dd\eta=0$ for $a+b>p$ and $i+j\le p$. That is to say, $\Cp_p$ is the orthogonal complement of the polynomials of total degree $p$ within $\Qp_p(\xi,\eta)$, which is unique and does not depend on any basis (\cref{app:derivations}).

For a folded element, the hybrid functions can still be evaluated at every quadrature point, since they are defined through the reference coordinates. Their values at physical points, however, are not defined where the map is not one-to-one. All element quantities are therefore computed in the reference coordinates, as in \cref{eq:Ke}.
%
\subsection{Properties}
\label{sec:hybrid-properties}
%
If the Vandermonde matrix of the hybrid functions is regular, the hybrid trial space has the following four properties (\cref{app:derivations}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] it consists of $n$ linearly independent functions, and the shape functions exist and are unique;
  \item[(ii)] it contains $\Pp_p(x,y)$, so that condition (C1) holds for all polynomial solutions of degree $p$, including the rigid-body modes;
  \item[(iii)] it is invariant under affine transformations of the element, i.e., under translation, rotation, scaling and shearing (\cref{sec:skew});
  \item[(iv)] if the element is a straight-sided quadrilateral (bilinear geometry map), the hybrid shape functions coincide with the parametric ones, and \PH\ is the conventional, symmetric spectral element.
\end{enumerate}
Property (iv) follows from the fact that $x$ and $y$ are bilinear functions of $(\xi,\eta)$ on such an element. Hence, every polynomial of total degree $p$ in $(x,y)$ is a polynomial of degree $p$ in each of $\xi$ and $\eta$, and both groups in \cref{eq:hybrid} lie in $\Qp_p(\xi,\eta)$. Since there are $n$ independent functions in a space of dimension $n$, the hybrid space is the whole space $\Qp_p(\xi,\eta)$ of the parametric functions.

Property (iv) means that the unsymmetric formulation becomes active only where it is needed, namely on elements with curved edges. On straight-sided meshes, \PH\ inherits the symmetry, the positive definiteness and the spectral properties of the conventional element, however strong the angular distortion may be. Since the conventional spectral element keeps its completeness under angular distortion (see \cref{sec:completeness}), nothing is lost on such meshes. The trial space $\Qsk$ of \PM\ does not have this property. In the companion paper, it was exactly on straight-sided elements with interior angles close to $180^\circ$ that the metric functions of \PM\ overshot most~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}.

\subsection{Hybrid interpolation as a corrected GLL interpolation}
\label{sec:hybrid-representation}
%
In order to understand why the hybrid trial functions overshoot much less than those of \PM, we compare both with the GLL interpolation $\Ihat$ of the conventional element, all functions being considered on the reference square. The hybrid interpolant $\mathcal I_Tf$ of a function $f$, i.e., the field of the hybrid trial functions with the nodal values of $f$, can be written as the GLL interpolant plus a correction (\cref{app:derivations}),
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal I_Tf=\Ihat f+\bigl(q-\Ihat q\bigr) .
  \label{eq:repT}
\end{equation}
%
Here, $q$ is the complete polynomial of degree $p$ in $(x,y)$ whose GLL interpolant has the same low-order Legendre coefficients ($a+b\le p$) as the GLL interpolant of $f$. In practice, $q$ is computed from the nodal values of $f$ by solving a small system with the $n_p\times n_p$ matrix $\matr S$, where $n_p=(p+1)(p+2)/2$ is the number of complete polynomials. The column $k$ of $\matr S$ contains the low-order Legendre coefficients of the GLL interpolant of the $k$-th complete polynomial. The correction $q-\Ihat q$ in \cref{eq:repT} is the GLL interpolation error of a complete polynomial of degree $p$ and thus precisely the part of $q$ that the conventional element cannot represent. We therefore call it the \emph{completeness defect} of the conventional element. If the conventional element is complete, in particular for every bilinear map, the correction vanishes, the hybrid interpolant is the GLL interpolant and $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}=\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$, independently of the amount of angular distortion.

The interpolant of \PM\ can be written in the same form, $\mathcal I_Qf=\Ihat f+\bigl(v-\Ihat v\bigr)$, where $v$ is now the function of the whole trial space $\Qsk$ with the nodal values of $f$ (\cref{app:derivations}). This isolates the structural difference between the two metric trial spaces. For \PH, the correction is restricted to the $n_p$ complete polynomials and involves only the inverse of the small matrix $\matr S$. The high-order part of the GLL interpolant remains untouched. For \PM, in contrast, the correction acts on all $n$ functions. It involves the GLL interpolation error of Cartesian polynomials of degree $p$ in each skew coordinate, i.e., of total degree up to $2p$, as well as the inverse of a full $n\times n$ matrix. In particular, this correction does not vanish on straight-sided elements, since $\bar\xi^a\bar\eta^b$ with $a+b>p$ is in general not contained in $\Qp_p(\xi,\eta)$ there. For a curved element that is a small perturbation of size $\delta$ of a straight-sided one, the correction of \PH\ is of the order of $\delta$. Both representations lead to upper bounds of the Lebesgue constants in terms of $\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ and the size of the correction (\cref{app:derivations}). These bounds are, however, not sharp enough to quantify the difference in advance. The numerical results of \cref{sec:num-lebesgue} show that $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}$ stays at or very close to $\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ for moderate edge curvature. For strong distortion, $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}$ grows as well, but by orders of magnitude less than $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}$, which grows exponentially in $p$ and in the distortion.

The matrix $\matr S$ also decides whether the hybrid shape functions exist. Let $\matr V_{\mathrm{ref}}$ be the Legendre Vandermonde matrix of the GLL grid of the reference square, which is fixed and well conditioned, and $\matr V_{\mathrm{T}}$ the Vandermonde matrix of the hybrid functions. Then (\cref{app:derivations})
%
\begin{equation}
  |\det(\matr V_{\mathrm{T}})|=|\det(\matr V_{\mathrm{ref}})|\,|\det(\matr S)| .
  \label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
%
Hence, the hybrid shape functions exist if and only if $\matr S$ is regular, and they exist on every element that is sufficiently close to a parallelogram, for which $\matr S$ is regular. Consequently, the reciprocal condition number $\mathrm{rcond}(\matr S)$ is a cheap element-level indicator, computed from an $n_p\times n_p$ instead of an $n\times n$ matrix. On strongly non-affine (folded) elements, however, the existence is not guaranteed. In \cref{sec:num-cylinder}, we show that it fails on some folded elements for $p\ge7$, exactly where $\matr S$ becomes singular.

\subsection{Accuracy of the interpolation}
\label{sec:hybrid-approximation}
%
The best fit in \cref{eq:quasiopt} is at least as accurate as the interpolant, which is a trial field with the exact nodal values. For the interpolant $\mathcal I_{\mathcal U}f$ of a smooth field $f$ by the trial functions of \PM\ or \PH, the following estimates hold (\cref{app:derivations}):
%
\begin{equation}
  \max_{K}\bigl|f-\mathcal I_{\mathcal U}f\bigr|\le C\,(1+\Lam_{\mathcal U})\,h_K^{p+1}\,|f|_{p+1}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \max_{K}\bigl|\nabla(f-\mathcal I_{\mathcal U}f)\bigr|\le C\,(1+\Lam'_{\mathcal U})\,h_K^{p}\,|f|_{p+1} .
  \label{eq:interp}
\end{equation}
%
Here, $h_K$ is the diameter of the element, $|f|_{p+1}$ the largest $(p+1)$-th derivative of $f$ on the convex hull of the element, $C$ a constant that depends only on $p$, and
%
\begin{equation}
  \Lam'_{\mathcal U}=h_K\max_{\bx\in K}\sum_i\bigl|\nabla M_i^K(\bx)\bigr|
  \label{eq:lebesgue1}
\end{equation}
%
is the Lebesgue constant of the derivatives. The estimates follow from the reproduction of the complete polynomials: the interpolation error of $f$ equals that of the difference between $f$ and its Taylor polynomial of degree $p$, which is of the order of $h_K^{p+1}$. If a mesh is refined such that the element shapes do not change (self-similar refinement), $\Lam_{\mathcal U}$ and $\Lam'_{\mathcal U}$ do not depend on $h_K$.

Thus, both metric trial spaces have the optimal order of interpolation for the displacements and their derivatives, and hence in the energy norm, on meshes whose distortion persists under refinement, on which the conventional element is limited to $\Pp_{\lfloor p/q\rfloor}(x,y)$ (\cref{sec:completeness}). The two metric trial spaces differ in the constants $1+\Lam_{\mathcal U}$ and $1+\Lam'_{\mathcal U}$. Within \cref{eq:quasiopt}, these constants enter the best-fit term only. The stability factor $\beta_h$, on the other hand, depends on the pair of test and trial functions and is not covered by \cref{eq:interp}. An error estimate for the finite element solution would therefore require a discrete inf-sup condition for the pair of the conforming test functions and the discontinuous trial functions together with a consistency estimate in the sense of Strang. We do not prove such an estimate for either trial space. Instead, the stability is assessed numerically in \cref{sec:num-convergence,sec:num-spectra,sec:num-cylinder}.

\subsection{Gradients, stiffness matrix and folded elements}
\label{sec:hybrid-gradients}
%
For the functions of the first group in \cref{eq:hybrid}, the Cartesian gradient is given by $\nabla_{\bx}\phi=\matr J_0^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(r_{\xi}^{-1},r_{\eta}^{-1})\,\nabla_{\bxit}\phi$, as for \PM. For the functions of the complement, on the other hand, $\nabla_{\bx}\phi=\matr J(\bxi)^{-1}\nabla_{\bxi}\phi$ at the quadrature point $\bxi$, as for the conventional element. Hence, the stiffness matrix \cref{eq:Ke} requires no inverse map, and post-processing at arbitrary physical points requires $\bx_K^{-1}$ only as for the conventional element. Since $\matr J^{-1}$ contains $1/\det(\matr J)$, the complement contributes rational integrands to $\matr K_K$, as for curved isoparametric elements. This does not affect the exact reproduction of polynomial solutions. For a polynomial displacement field of degree $p$, the coefficients of the interpolant on the complement vanish, so that $\matr B_{\mathrm M}\vect d=\bm\varepsilon(\vect u)$, and the integrand $\bar{\matr B}_{\mathrm N}^\top\matr C\,\bm\varepsilon(\vect u)$ is the same polynomial as for \PM. Consequently, condition (C3) can be met by Gauss quadrature. Note that the sign of $\det(\matr J)$ is irrelevant for the function values. A vanishing $\det(\matr J)$ at a quadrature point, however, has to be excluded, since the gradients of the complement functions involve $\matr J^{-1}$. On a folded element, $\det(\matr J)$ changes sign along a curve inside the element, and the complement part of the integrand behaves like $1/\det(\matr J)$ near this curve. This singularity is not integrable. Consequently, the stiffness matrix of \PH, like that of the conventional element, does not exist on folded elements, and its Gauss approximations do not converge (\cref{sec:num-quadrature}). Only the product of the stiffness matrix with the nodal values of a polynomial field of degree $p$ remains well defined, since the complement does not contribute to it.
