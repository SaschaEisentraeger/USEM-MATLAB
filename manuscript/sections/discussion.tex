\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What each improvement achieves}
The two improvements act on different error sources and should not be conflated.
The Lagrange basis of \cref{sec:basis} removes the ill-conditioning \emph{of the
construction}: it is exact on affine elements, and on distorted ones it reduces the
condition number by orders of magnitude relative to monomials, while
\cref{prop:kappa} shows that the remainder stems from extrapolating the metric
interpolant to the bounding box of the element. It cannot
change the shape functions (\cref{prop:invariance}) and hence cannot change the
Lebesgue constant, the patch-test behaviour in exact arithmetic, the eigenvalues or
the discrete solution beyond round-off. The hybrid space of \cref{sec:hybrid} changes
the \emph{space}, and with it the Lebesgue constant, the stability properties and the
approximation constant. Both are needed: the hybrid space has its own Vandermonde
matrix, which in the Legendre-type basis used here is better conditioned than that of
$\Qp_p(s,t)$ in any basis for $p\ge4$ (by up to five orders of magnitude at $p=16$),
because the complement is evaluated on the exact GLL tensor grid, but whose condition
number still grows with $p$ ($\approx2\cdot10^{6}$ at $p=16$, \cref{fig:kappa}).

\paragraph{Trade-off between the two trial spaces}
For smooth solutions on meshes where $\Qp_p(s,t)$ is stable, \PM\ is
more accurate than \PH\ by a factor of $1.3$--$5.5$ in the energy norm, at the same
convergence order (\cref{sec:num-convergence}). The reason is the larger Cartesian
polynomial content of $\Qp_p(s,t)$. This advantage is lost abruptly when the Lebesgue
constant of $\Qp_p(s,t)$ becomes large: on the $\delta=0.2$ meshes \PM\ diverges for
$p\ge5$, and on all three $3\times2$ meshes it develops complex and negative eigenvalues
from $p=5$--$7$ on. Neither the degree nor the amount of distortion at which this happens
is known a priori; the Jacobian is not an indicator (the bent element has
$\det\matr J\equiv1$). \PH\ was stable and optimally convergent in every non-folded case
studied. In our view this makes $\Tp_p$ the appropriate default for high-order
unsymmetric spectral elements, in particular for transient, modal and frequency-domain
analyses, whereas $\Qp_p(s,t)$ remains attractive for static analyses at low to moderate
degree and distortion, where its larger Cartesian content pays off. An element-level
indicator for the latter choice is $\Lam_Q$ itself, which can be estimated by evaluating
the metric shape functions on a sampling grid in the element (the condition number of
$\matr V$ is not a substitute, since it also contains the extrapolation to the bounding
box, \cref{prop:kappa}).

\paragraph{Folded elements}
Exactness for polynomial solutions is observed on folded elements for both
spaces (\cref{rem:folded}), as the patch tests on the panel meshes of
\cref{sec:num-patch} confirm; at $p=8$, \PH\ reduces the round-off level there by one
to two orders of magnitude. For non-polynomial solutions on folded meshes, however, \PH\
converges less regularly than on valid meshes, and its energy error stagnates, because
the complement part of $\matr K_e$ contains $1/\det\matr J$, which is singular on the
curve $\det\matr J=0$ inside the element. A remedy would be to integrate these terms
with a rule adapted to the singular curve, or to write the complement gradients in a form
that avoids $\matr J^{-1}$ (e.g.\ by multiplying the complement functions by $\det\matr J$,
which changes the space); we have not pursued this. On folded meshes \PM\ is unstable at
high order in our tests, and \PP\ converges with reduced order in the nodal error.

\paragraph{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item No a priori stability (discrete inf-sup) estimate is available for either metric
  space; the statements on stability rest on the computed spectra, condition numbers
  and convergence histories. \Cref{prop:representation} gives exact representations and
  upper bounds of the Lebesgue constants, but the size of the correction operators is
  quantified numerically only.
\item The completeness degree of $\Tp_p$ is $\min(p_\xi,p_\eta)$, the same as for
  $\Qp_{p_\xi,p_\eta}(s,t)$; neither space reproduces $\Pp_{\max(p_\xi,p_\eta)}$.
\item The complement contributes rational integrands to $\matr K_e$ and
  $\matr M_e$; Gauss quadrature is then not exact, as for curved isoparametric
  elements. Where $\det\matr J$ vanishes inside an element, the integrands are singular.
\item The study is restricted to linear plane elasticity on quadrilaterals with at most
  biquadratic geometry. The construction carries over to hexahedra
  ($\Pp_p(x,y,z)\oplus\Cp_p$ with the $L^2$-complement of $\Pp_p(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ in
  $\Qp_p(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$) and to triangles (complement in a simplex basis), but has not
  been tested there.
\end{itemize}
