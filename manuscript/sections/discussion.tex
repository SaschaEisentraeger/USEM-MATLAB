\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%
\emph{Effect of the two improvements.} The two improvements act on different sources of error and therefore have to be assessed separately. The Lagrange basis of \cref{sec:basis} removes the ill-conditioning of the \emph{construction}. It is exact on parallelograms, and on distorted elements it reduces the condition number by orders of magnitude compared with monomials. The remainder stems from the extrapolation of the metric shape functions to the bounding box of the element (\cref{eq:kappa}). The basis cannot change the shape functions (\cref{sec:basis-invariance}). Consequently, it cannot change the Lebesgue constant, the patch-test behavior in exact arithmetic, the eigenvalues or the finite element solution beyond round-off. The hybrid trial space of \cref{sec:hybrid}, in contrast, changes the shape functions and with them the Lebesgue constant, the stability and the approximation constant. Both improvements are needed, since the hybrid trial space has its own Vandermonde matrix. In the Legendre-type basis used here, this matrix is better conditioned than that of $\Qsk$ in any basis for $p\ge4$, by up to five orders of magnitude at $p=16$, since the complement is evaluated on the exact GLL tensor grid. Its condition number still grows with $p$ (about $2\cdot10^{6}$ at $p=16$, \cref{fig:kappa}).

\emph{Approximation and stability.} In terms of \cref{eq:quasiopt}, the Lebesgue constant enters the error twice. It bounds the interpolation error and with it the best fit (\cref{eq:interp}), and our results indicate that it is accompanied by a loss of stability of the unsymmetric system. On the meshes with $\delta=0.2$, the interpolation error of the trial functions of \PM\ decreases with the optimal order, since the element shapes do not change under refinement, whereas the finite element solution of \PM\ diverges for $p=5,6$. For the thick cylinder, the \PM\ solution is up to 300 times worse than its interpolant. Thus, the loss of accuracy of the original metric trial functions observed in the companion paper~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric} is not only an approximation effect but enters through the factor $1+1/\beta_h$ as well. For \PH, the ratio of the discretization error to the interpolation error was $0.78$--$1.00$ in all cases without folded elements, and no eigenvalue with negative real part occurred on any mesh. Note, however, that we have neither computed $\beta_h$ nor proven a lower bound for it. The statements on stability rest on the spectra, the convergence histories and the comparison with the interpolant.

\emph{Trade-off between the two trial spaces.} For smooth solutions on meshes on which \PM\ is stable, \PM\ is more accurate than \PH\ by a factor of $1.3$--$5.6$ in the energy norm, at the same convergence order (\cref{sec:num-convergence}). This is due to the larger Cartesian polynomial content of $\Qsk$. This advantage is, however, lost abruptly when the Lebesgue constant of $\Qsk$ becomes large. On the meshes with $\delta=0.2$, \PM\ diverges for $p\ge5$, and on all three $3\times2$ meshes it develops complex eigenvalues with negative real part from $p=5$--$7$ on. Neither the degree nor the amount of distortion at which this happens is known in advance, and the Jacobian is not an indicator either, since the bent element has $\det(\matr J)\equiv1$. \PH\ was stable and converged with the optimal order in every case without folded elements that we studied. In our opinion, this makes the hybrid trial space the appropriate default for unsymmetric spectral elements of high degree, in particular for transient, modal and frequency-domain analyses. The trial space $\Qsk$, on the other hand, remains attractive for static analyses at low to moderate degree and distortion, where its larger Cartesian content pays off. An element-level indicator for this choice is $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}$ itself, which can be estimated by evaluating the metric shape functions on a sampling grid in the element. Note that the condition number of $\matr V$ is not a substitute, since it also contains the extrapolation to the bounding box (\cref{eq:kappa}).

\emph{Consequences for the companion paper.} In the companion study~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}, the metric trial functions lost accuracy on two kinds of elements, namely on straight-sided elements with interior angles close to $180^\circ$ and on strongly curved elements. For the first kind, property (iv) of \cref{sec:hybrid-properties} settles the question: on straight-sided elements, \PH\ is the conventional spectral element, which keeps its completeness and, as observed in the companion paper, its rates under angular distortion. The loss of rate of \PM\ for $p=4,5$ on angularly distorted meshes and its stagnation under $p$-refinement therefore cannot occur for \PH. For the second kind, the element with one strongly curved edge ($c=1$, $p=8$) gives $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}=65$ instead of $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}=4.6\cdot10^{4}$, which suggests that the hybrid trial space also improves the strongly curved head elements of the pressure vessel. Moreover, the enrichment of the companion paper relies only on the exact reproduction of the polynomials up to the complete degree, which holds for the hybrid trial space as well. While the first consequence follows directly from the properties of the hybrid space, the other two are expectations that have not been computed in the paper at hand, and the axisymmetric case with its hoop strain has not been tested.

\emph{Folded elements.} Exactness for polynomial solutions is observed on folded elements for both unsymmetric elements, as the patch tests on the panel meshes of \cref{sec:num-patch} confirm, although the argument of \cref{sec:exact} does not cover them. At $p=8$, \PH\ reduces the round-off level there by one to two orders of magnitude. For non-polynomial solutions, however, the calibration of the Gauss rules revealed that the stiffness matrices of \PH\ and of the conventional element do not exist on folded elements, since their integrands contain the non-integrable factor $1/\det(\matr J)$ (\cref{sec:hybrid-gradients}). Their results then depend on the quadrature rule: with $2p$ points, \PH\ appears to converge in the nodal error on the folded square meshes, whereas with the reference rule it even diverges for $p=2$. The apparent convergence is thus an artifact of the rule. Only the unsymmetric element with the trial space $\Qsk$ keeps well-defined element matrices on folded elements, which is the property emphasized by Rajendran~\cite{Rajendran2010technique}, but in our tests it is unstable at high degree there. A hybrid trial space suitable for folded elements would have to avoid $\matr J^{-1}$ in the complement gradients, e.g., by multiplying the complement functions by $\det(\matr J)$, which changes the space. This is out of the scope of this contribution and left for future research.

\emph{Limitations.} Several limitations of the present study have to be kept in mind. First, no discrete inf-sup condition~\cite{Babuska1971Error} is available for either metric trial space. While \cref{eq:repT} and its counterpart for \PM\ give exact representations and upper bounds of the Lebesgue constants, the size of the corrections is only quantified numerically. Second, for anisotropic degrees $p_\xi\ne p_\eta$, the complete degree of the hybrid trial space is $\min(p_\xi,p_\eta)$, the same as for $\Qp_{p_\xi,p_\eta}(\bar\xi,\bar\eta)$, and neither space reproduces the complete polynomials of degree $\max(p_\xi,p_\eta)$. Third, the complement contributes rational integrands to $\matr K_K$, so that Gauss quadrature is not exact, as for curved isoparametric elements. The calibrated rules make the quadrature error negligible on all meshes without folded elements, but they require up to $51$ points per direction on nearly degenerate elements, which is considerably more than the $2p$ points that are sufficient for \PM\ in the patch tests. On folded elements, the integrals of \PH\ do not exist. Fourth, all distorted meshes of this study have a curvature that persists under refinement. A refinement series in which $\delta$ decreases with the element size, as for a resolved curved geometry, was not computed. According to the companion paper, the conventional element converges with the optimal rate on such meshes, and the unsymmetric formulation gains only a constant factor of at most about 2 for $p\le5$~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}. The undistorted thick-cylinder meshes ($\alpha=0^\circ$), on which the three formulations are equivalent, are consistent with this. Finally, the study is restricted to linear plane elasticity on quadrilaterals with at most biquadratic geometry. The construction carries over to hexahedra, with the orthogonal complement of the complete polynomials of degree $p$ in $(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ within the tensor-product polynomials, and to triangles, with a complement in a simplex basis, but it has not been tested there.
