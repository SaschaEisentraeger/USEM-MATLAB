\section{Unsymmetric spectral elements}
\label{sec:formulation}
%
In this section, we briefly summarize the unsymmetric (Petrov-Galerkin) spectral element and the properties of the metric trial functions on which the remainder of the paper relies. For the sake of self-containedness, the main ingredients of the companion paper~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric} are recalled for plane elasticity.
%
\subsection{Discretization with different test and trial functions}
\label{sec:pg}
%
We consider a linear elastic body in plane stress or plane strain that occupies the domain $\Omega$. With the strain vector $\bm\varepsilon=[\varepsilon_{xx},\varepsilon_{yy},\gamma_{xy}]^\top$ and the elasticity matrix $\matr C$, the internal and external virtual work read
%
\begin{equation}
  a(\vect u,\vect v)=\int\limits_{\Omega}\bm\varepsilon(\vect v)^\top\matr C\,\bm\varepsilon(\vect u)\dd A\qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \ell(\vect v)=\int\limits_{\Omega}\vect v\cdot\vect b\dd A+\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm N}}\vect v\cdot\bar{\vect t}\dd s ,
  \label{eq:forms}
\end{equation}
%
where $\vect u$ denotes the displacement field, $\vect v$ a virtual displacement field, $\vect b$ the body force and $\bar{\vect t}$ the traction prescribed on the Neumann boundary $\Gamma_{\mathrm N}$. The exact displacement field satisfies the principle of virtual work $a(\vect u,\vect v)=\ell(\vect v)$ for all admissible virtual displacements $\vect v$. We measure the error of an approximate solution in the energy norm $\enorm{\vect v}$, whose square $\enorm{\vect v}^2=a(\vect v,\vect v)$ is twice the strain energy of the field $\vect v$.

The domain is divided into quadrilaterals $K$, which are the images of the reference square $\Kh=[-1,1]^2$ under the geometry map $\bx_K(\xi,\eta)$. The map is a polynomial of degree $q$ in each reference coordinate. In this paper, the geometry is described by 8-node (quadratic Serendipity) elements, so that $q\le2$, and the elements of degree $p>2$ are subparametric. The $n=(p+1)^2$ nodes of an element are the images $\bx_j=\bx_K(\bxi_j)$ of the GLL points $\bxi_j$ of the reference square. On $\Kh$, we use the tensor-product Lagrange polynomials $\hat N_i(\xi,\eta)$ through these points, i.e., the shape functions of the spectral element~\cite{Patera1984spectral} in its Legendre form~\cite{Ronquist1987legendre}, see also the textbook of Karniadakis \& Sherwin~\cite{Karniadakis2005spectral}. They span the space $\Qp_p(\xi,\eta)$ of all polynomials of degree $p$ in each reference coordinate. Mapped to the physical element, these \emph{parametric} shape functions $N_i$ are continuous across element edges. They serve as \emph{test functions} for the virtual displacements $\vect v_h=\sum\limits_{i}N_i\,\delta\vect d_i$, which are therefore admissible virtual displacements of the continuous problem.

The displacements themselves are approximated with \emph{trial functions} $M_i^K$ that interpolate the same nodal values, $M_i^K(\bx_j)=\delta_{ij}$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta. Hence, the displacement field in element $K$ is $\vect u_h|_K=\sum\limits_{i}M_i^K\,\vect d_i$ with the nodal displacements $\vect d_i$, which are shared by neighboring elements. Note that the trial functions need not be continuous across element edges, since only the virtual displacements have to be admissible~\cite{Rajendran2003novel}. Therefore, the internal virtual work is evaluated element by element, $a_h(\vect u_h,\vect v_h)=\sum\limits_{K}a_K(\vect u_h,\vect v_h)$, where $a_K$ is the contribution of element $K$ to $a$. The discrete equations read
%
\begin{equation}
  a_h(\vect u_h,\vect v_h)=\ell(\vect v_h)\qquad\text{for all test functions }\vect v_h ,
  \label{eq:pg}
\end{equation}
%
with the nodal displacements prescribed on the Dirichlet boundary. The element stiffness matrix is evaluated with the adjugate of the Jacobian matrix in the test functions,
%
\begin{equation}
  \matr K_K=\int\limits_{\Kh}\bar{\matr B}_{\mathrm N}^\top\,\matr C\,\matr B_{\mathrm M}\dd\xi\dd\eta\qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \bar{\matr B}_{\mathrm N}=\det(\matr J)\,\matr B_{\mathrm N} ,
  \label{eq:Ke}
\end{equation}
%
where $\matr B_{\mathrm N}$ and $\matr B_{\mathrm M}$ are the strain-displacement matrices of the test and the trial functions, respectively, and $\matr J=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}x_{,\xi}&y_{,\xi}\\x_{,\eta}&y_{,\eta}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ is the Jacobian matrix. Since $\bar{\matr B}_{\mathrm N}$ is assembled from $\adj(\matr J)\,\nabla_{\bxi}\hat N_i$ instead of $\matr J^{-1}\nabla_{\bxi}\hat N_i=\adj(\matr J)\,\nabla_{\bxi}\hat N_i/\det(\matr J)$, $\det(\matr J)$ does not appear in the denominator of the test part~\cite{Rajendran2010technique}. The consistent mass matrix is $\matr M_K=\int\limits_{\Kh}\rho\,\matr N_{\mathrm N}^\top\matr N_{\mathrm M}\det(\matr J)\dd\xi\dd\eta$, where $\rho$ is the mass density and $\matr N_{\mathrm N}$ and $\matr N_{\mathrm M}$ contain the values of the test and the trial functions. With GLL quadrature, it is diagonal, since both families are interpolatory at the GLL points. In the following, we denote by \PP\ the conventional (symmetric) spectral element with $M_i^K=N_i$, by \PM\ the unsymmetric element with the metric trial functions of the companion paper (\cref{sec:skew}) and by \PH\ the unsymmetric element with the hybrid trial functions introduced in \cref{sec:hybrid}.
%
\subsection{Exact reproduction and error of a general solution}
\label{sec:exact}
%
The distortion immunity of unsymmetric elements rests on the following argument~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}, cf.\ the sufficient conditions in Ref.~\cite{Rajendran2010technique}. The exact solution satisfies the principle of virtual work for every admissible virtual displacement, in particular for every test function of \cref{eq:pg}, since the test functions are conforming. Suppose now that the following four conditions hold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(C1)] \emph{Representation:} in every element, the exact displacement field can be represented by the trial functions, with the exact nodal displacements as coefficients;
  \item[(C2)] \emph{Boundary data:} the prescribed nodal displacements are exact;
  \item[(C3)] \emph{Integration:} all integrals of the stiffness matrix and the load vector are evaluated exactly;
  \item[(C4)] \emph{Regularity:} the system of equations has a unique solution.
\end{enumerate}
Then the nodal displacements of the exact solution satisfy \cref{eq:pg}, and since the solution is unique, the finite element solution coincides with the exact solution, $\vect u_h=\vect u$. Note that nothing in this argument depends on the shape of the elements. Whether a formulation is distortion-immune for a class of solutions is therefore decided by condition (C1), up to quadrature. That is to say, the class has to be contained in the trial space (Ansatz space) $\mathcal U_K$ of every element, i.e., in the set of displacement fields that the trial functions of the element can represent. In the following, $\Pp_k(x,y)$ denotes the complete polynomials of degree $k$ in the physical coordinates, i.e., all linear combinations of $x^ay^b$ with $a+b\le k$. If $\Pp_k(x,y)$ is contained in the trial space of every element, every polynomial solution of degree $k$ is reproduced on arbitrarily distorted meshes. In particular, the trial space has to contain $\Pp_1(x,y)$, which comprises the rigid-body modes and the constant strain states.

If an element map is not one-to-one, i.e., if $\det(\matr J)$ changes sign within the element, we call the element \emph{folded}. On a folded element, the parametric functions are not well defined in physical space, and the above argument does not apply. Moreover, the stiffness integrals exist there only for \PM\ (\cref{sec:hybrid-gradients}). For such elements, exact reproduction of polynomial solutions was observed for low-order elements by Rajendran~\cite{Rajendran2010technique}, and it is confirmed by the patch tests of \cref{sec:num-patch}. We do not give a proof.

A general solution is not contained in the trial space. Since the trial field can be discontinuous, its error is measured in the energy norm evaluated element by element, $\enormh{\vect v}^2=\sum\limits_{K}a_K(\vect v,\vect v)$, which is often called the broken energy norm and coincides with $\enorm{\vect v}$ for continuous fields. Following the argument of Babu\v{s}ka~\cite[Theorem~2.2]{Babuska1971Error}, which carries over to the discontinuous trial functions, since the exact solution satisfies the discrete equations with the conforming test functions~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}, the error is bounded by
%
\begin{equation}
  \enormh{\vect u-\vect u_h}\;\le\;\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\beta_h}\Bigr)\min_{\vect w_h}\enormh{\vect u-\vect w_h} .
  \label{eq:quasiopt}
\end{equation}
%
Here, the minimum is taken over all trial fields $\vect w_h$ with the prescribed nodal values, so that it is the error of the best fit that the trial functions can achieve. The factor $\beta_h>0$ is the stability factor of the unsymmetric system, i.e., a measure of how well the test functions control the trial functions. A small $\beta_h$ means that this control is weak, so that the error can be much larger than that of the best fit. For the symmetric formulation, $\beta_h=1$. For unsymmetric elements, however, $\beta_h$ depends on the trial and test functions and on the element shapes, and we do not have a proof that it stays above a fixed positive value under mesh refinement. Therefore, we use \cref{eq:quasiopt} only to interpret the numerical results. It shows that the error of a general solution consists of two factors: the error of the best fit, which is controlled by the completeness and the interpolation properties of the trial functions (\cref{sec:hybrid-approximation}), and the factor $1+1/\beta_h$. A large Lebesgue constant of the trial functions can enlarge the former, and an unstable pair of test and trial functions the latter. The thick-cylinder study in \cref{sec:num-cylinder} separates the two numerically by comparing the finite element solution with the interpolant of the exact solution by the same trial functions.

\subsection{Completeness of the mapped spectral element}
\label{sec:completeness}
%
For a geometry map of degree $q$ in each reference coordinate, a monomial $x^ay^b$ becomes a polynomial of degree $q(a+b)$ in each of $\xi$ and $\eta$, which is contained in $\Qp_p(\xi,\eta)$ only if $q(a+b)\le p$. Thus, on a curved element with the 8-node geometry ($q=2$), the conventional spectral element represents the complete polynomials only up to degree $\lfloor p/2\rfloor$. For the bilinear map of a straight-sided quadrilateral ($q=1$), in contrast, the mapped shape functions contain $\Pp_p(x,y)$. That is to say, spectral elements, unlike Serendipity elements, keep their completeness under angular distortion~\cite{Lee1993effects,Arnold2002Approximation}.

Whether the loss of completeness reduces the convergence rate depends on how the curvature behaves under refinement. To this end, a curved edge is described by its chord length $L$ and its sagitta $f$, i.e., the largest distance between the edge and its chord. For the 8-node geometry, $f$ is the distance by which the mid-side node is moved normal to the chord, and the distortion measure is the relative sagitta
%
\begin{equation}
  \delta=\frac{f}{L} .
  \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
%
If $\delta$ is fixed under refinement, the part of a smooth solution that the conventional element misses decreases only with $h^{\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1}$, where $h$ denotes the element size. Consequently, the energy-norm error converges with $h^{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}$ instead of $h^p$~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}. If, however, a curved boundary is resolved by refinement, $\delta$ decreases in proportion to the element size, the missed part is of the order of the truncation error, and the optimal rate is retained~\cite{Ciarlet1972Interpolation,Lenoir1986optimal}. Meshes of a resolved curved geometry therefore do not reveal the loss of completeness. A fixed $\delta$, on the other hand, represents elements whose edges are curved for reasons unrelated to a resolved geometry, e.g., by high-order mesh generation with curved interior edges, by untangling or by the deformation of a curved mesh. The distorted meshes of \cref{sec:numerics} are of this type.

\subsection{Metric shape functions in skew coordinates}
\label{sec:skew}
%
In the original element of Rajendran \& Liew~\cite{Rajendran2003novel}, the metric functions are polynomials in the global Cartesian coordinates, and their incomplete terms make the element stiffness matrix depend on the orientation of the element~\cite{Ooi2008Remedies}. For the spectral element, this problem is even more pronounced, since the tensor-product monomials of degree $p$ in each coordinate contain incomplete terms up to the total degree $2p$. Therefore, we follow Xie et al.~\cite{Xie2016Modified} and the companion paper and write the metric functions in the skew coordinates of Yuan et al.~\cite{Yuan1993New},
%
\begin{equation}
  \bxib=\begin{pmatrix}\bar\xi\\\bar\eta\end{pmatrix}=\matr J_0^{-\top}(\bx-\bx_0),\qquad
  \bx_0=\bx_K(\vect 0),\qquad
  \matr J_0=\matr J\big|_{\xi=\eta=0},
  \label{eq:skew}
\end{equation}
%
where $\bx_0$ is the position of the element center and $\matr J_0$ the Jacobian matrix there, which has to be regular. On a parallelogram, the geometry map is affine, and $\bxib$ coincides with the reference coordinates $\bxi=(\xi,\eta)$. On all other elements, $\bxib$ is an affine approximation of $\bxi$ that is exact at the element center. If the element is moved, rotated, scaled or sheared by an affine transformation $\bx\mapsto\matr A\bx+\vect c$ with $\det(\matr A)\ne0$, the skew coordinates of every material point remain unchanged, since $\bx-\bx_0$ and $\matr J_0^\top$ are both multiplied by $\matr A$, which cancels in \cref{eq:skew}. Consequently, every function written in $(\bar\xi,\bar\eta)$ or in $(\xi,\eta)$, and with it every trial space of this paper, is independent of the position, the orientation and the size of the element.

The trial space of \PM\ is $\mathcal U_K=\Qsk$, i.e., all polynomials of degree $p$ in each skew coordinate. Since $\bxib$ is an affine function of $\bx$, this space contains $\Pp_p(x,y)$, and condition (C1) holds for all polynomial solutions of degree $p$ on every element. On a parallelogram, $\bxib=\bxi$, so that the metric and the parametric functions coincide and \PM\ reduces to \PP. On all other elements, the two differ, also on straight-sided ones, although the parametric functions already contain $\Pp_p(x,y)$ there.

The metric shape functions are computed by nodal interpolation. Given a basis $\{\phi_c\}_{c=1}^n$ of the trial space, e.g., the monomials $\bar\xi^a\bar\eta^b$ with $0\le a,b\le p$, the shape functions at the query points $\bx_r$, e.g., the images of the quadrature points, follow from the generalized Vandermonde matrix $\matr V$ and the basis values $\matr P$ at the query points,
%
\begin{equation}
  V_{cj}=\phi_c(\bx_j),\qquad P_{cr}=\phi_c(\bx_r)\qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \matr N_{\mathrm M}=\matr V^{-1}\matr P ,
  \label{eq:vandermonde}
\end{equation}
%
where $(\matr N_{\mathrm M})_{ir}=M_i^K(\bx_r)$ is the value of the shape function of node $i$ at the query point $\bx_r$. The derivatives are obtained analogously from the derivatives of the basis functions. In the companion paper and in the original construction~\cite{Rajendran2003novel,Ooi2006unsymmetric}, $\phi_c$ are the monomials. The metric functions of two neighboring elements are interpolants in different skew coordinates. Along the common edge, they agree at the edge nodes but not between them, so that the trial field is discontinuous across element edges unless both elements are parallelograms. Note that no trial functions that contain $\Pp_p(x,y)$ on curved elements can be continuous with nodal unknowns alone. On a parabolic edge, the polynomials of degree $p$ in $(x,y)$ reduce to polynomials of degree $2p$ in the edge parameter, which $p+1$ edge nodes cannot determine~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}. This affects neither the exactness argument nor \cref{eq:quasiopt}, which rely only on the conformity of the test functions.

How strongly the trial functions overshoot between the nodes is measured by the Lebesgue function and the Lebesgue constant,
%
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\mathcal U}(\bx)=\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}\bigl|M_i^K(\bx)\bigr|\qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \Lam_{\mathcal U}=\max_{\bx\in K}\lambda_{\mathcal U}(\bx) .
  \label{eq:lebesgue}
\end{equation}
%
That is to say, $\Lam_{\mathcal U}$ is the largest value that the trial field of the element can take if all nodal values lie between $-1$ and $1$. For well-behaved shape functions, it is a small number, e.g., $3.2$ for the GLL shape functions of degree $p=5$. The Lebesgue constant is also the factor by which the nodal interpolation can amplify an error. If the nodal values of a smooth field $f$ are interpolated, the largest error of the interpolant in the element is at most $1+\Lam_{\mathcal U}$ times the largest error of the best possible fit of $f$ by the trial functions~\cite{Brutman1997Lebesgue}. Likewise, an error in the nodal values, e.g., round-off or the nodal error of a finite element solution, is amplified by up to $\Lam_{\mathcal U}$ between the nodes. In the remainder of this article, $\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ denotes the Lebesgue constant of the GLL shape functions of \PP, and $\Lam_{\mathrm{Q}}$ and $\Lam_{\mathrm{T}}$ those of the trial functions of \PM\ and \PH, respectively. Note that $\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$ does not depend on the element shape, since the parametric functions are the GLL shape functions on the reference square. For the metric functions, in contrast, the Lebesgue constant grows strongly on elements whose nodes do not form a tensor-product grid in the skew coordinates~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}.
