\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%
Isoparametric finite elements lose accuracy when the element shape departs from the reference square. The reason is the nonlinear map from the reference square to a non-affine element: shape functions that are polynomials in the reference coordinates are, in general, no longer polynomials in the physical coordinates. Therefore, an element of degree $p$ does not necessarily represent all polynomials of degree $p$ in $(x,y)$, i.e., it loses completeness~\cite{Rajendran2000completeness}. Lee \& Bathe~\cite{Lee1993effects} showed that angular distortion already reduces the completeness of Serendipity elements to the linear terms, whereas Lagrange elements retain it under angular, but not under curved-edge distortion. For straight-sided quadrilaterals, Arnold et al.~\cite{Arnold2002Approximation} proved that the full tensor-product polynomials in the reference coordinates are necessary for the optimal order of approximation. For curved elements, the situation is worse: a geometry map of degree $q$ in each reference coordinate turns a polynomial of degree $k$ in the physical coordinates into one of degree $qk$ in the reference coordinates. Consequently, a spectral element of degree $p$ with a quadratic geometry ($q=2$) represents the complete polynomials only up to degree $\lfloor p/2\rfloor$. MacNeal~\cite{MacNeal1992On} showed that no element with a symmetric stiffness matrix can both pass the patch test and be free of locking for general element shapes.

The unsymmetric finite element method of Rajendran \& Liew~\cite{Rajendran2003novel} avoids this restriction by giving up the symmetry of the stiffness matrix. The test functions (virtual displacements) are the conventional parametric shape functions, which are continuous across element edges. The trial functions (displacements), on the other hand, are metric shape functions that interpolate the same nodal values and are complete in the physical coordinates. Rajendran~\cite{Rajendran2010technique} formulated sufficient conditions for distortion immunity, namely conforming test functions, trial functions that are complete in the physical coordinates and an element stiffness integrand without the Jacobian determinant in the denominator. Elements that satisfy them reproduce every displacement field of the complete degree on arbitrarily distorted meshes, and Rajendran~\cite{Rajendran2010technique} observed this numerically even on elements whose Jacobian determinant is negative in parts of the element. Since the incomplete terms of the original Cartesian basis make the element depend on its orientation~\cite{Ooi2008Remedies}, the metric functions were later written in a local frame~\cite{Ooi2008Remedies,Cowan2014Rotationally}. Xie et al.~\cite{Xie2016Modified} used the skew coordinates of Yuan et al.~\cite{Yuan1993New} instead, which are defined by the Jacobian matrix at the element center. In the wide body of literature, the approach has been extended to hexahedral~\cite{Ooi2004hexahedron} and triangular~\cite{Liew2006quadratic} elements, to analytical trial functions~\cite{Cen2012shape,Cen2015unsymmetric,Cen2019Some}, to orthotropic and incompatible-mode elements~\cite{Shang2018airy,Huang2022Unsymmetric}, to enhanced assumed strains~\cite{Pfefferkorn2021mesh,Pfefferkorn2023hourglassing}, to geometrically nonlinear problems~\cite{Ooi2007extension,Li2018high} and to elastodynamics~\cite{Wang2022Temporal,Zaehringer2026On}, to name just a few. The non-variational character of the formulation was analyzed by Prathap et al.~\cite{Prathap2006mesh,Prathap2007The}, and the gain in accuracy of unsymmetric Serendipity elements was related to the cost of solving the unsymmetric systems by Eisentr\"ager et al.~\cite{Eisentraeger2025unsymmetric}.

Almost all of this work concerns low-order elements with 4 to 20 nodes. In a companion paper~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}, we constructed unsymmetric spectral elements (USEM) of arbitrary degree $p$ for axisymmetric solids. Their test functions are the Lagrange polynomials through the Gauss-Lobatto-Legendre (GLL) points~\cite{Patera1984spectral,Ronquist1987legendre,Karniadakis2005spectral}. Their trial functions are the metric functions of Rajendran \& Liew~\cite{Rajendran2003novel}, built from the polynomials of degree $p$ in each skew coordinate of Xie et al.~\cite{Xie2016Modified} by inverting a generalized Vandermonde matrix of monomials. On meshes whose edge curvature persists under refinement, the conventional spectral element (SEM) with an 8-node geometry converged only with the rate $\lfloor p/2\rfloor$ in the energy norm, and on $128\times128$ elements its error was up to 6800 times that on a regular mesh. In contrast, USEM converged with the optimal rate $p$ and errors within 30\,\% of the regular-mesh errors. The same study, however, revealed a weakness of the metric trial functions. On strongly distorted straight-sided and on strongly curved elements, they overshoot between the nodes, increasingly with the degree. This overshoot is measured by the Lebesgue constant, i.e., the largest value that the interpolated field can take for nodal values between $-1$ and $1$. For $p=5$, the Lebesgue constant of the metric interpolation reached $906$ on a straight-sided element with an interior angle of $167.9^\circ$ and $2.0\cdot10^4$ on a strongly curved element, compared with $3.2$ for the parametric functions. Consequently, USEM lost the optimal rate on angularly distorted meshes for $p=4,5$, stagnated under $p$-refinement on these meshes, and on a coarse mesh of a pressure vessel its pole displacement deviated by $61\,\%$, compared with $3.1\,\%$ for SEM. Improving the robustness of the metric shape functions was therefore left for a future contribution, together with the suggestion to use the metric functions only on elements with curved edges~\cite{Eisentraeger2026axisymmetric}.

The paper at hand is this contribution. Here, we consider linear plane elasticity, so that the metric shape functions are examined without the hoop strain and the enrichment of the axisymmetric case. At high degree, two difficulties of a different nature have to be distinguished. The first is the ill-conditioning of the \emph{construction}. The Vandermonde matrix of the monomials is ill-conditioned already on undistorted elements, and its condition number grows exponentially with $p$~\cite{Gautschi1988Lower}. Consequently, the computed shape functions carry round-off errors of the order of the condition number times the machine precision. These errors destroy, e.g., the symmetry of the stiffness matrix on parallelograms and the Kronecker property on which the diagonal GLL mass matrix relies. The second is the ill-conditioning of the \emph{interpolation problem} itself. On non-affine elements, the nodes do not form a tensor-product grid in the skew coordinates, and the Lebesgue constant grows exponentially with $p$ and with the distortion. Note that this is a property of the trial space and of the nodes, independent of how the shape functions are computed. As we show in \cref{sec:numerics}, it is accompanied by a loss of stability of the unsymmetric system: complex eigenvalues with negative real part appear, which make the equations of motion unstable, and the static solution diverges under mesh refinement. Therefore, the overarching goal of this paper is to present two improvements of the metric shape functions that address these two difficulties separately.

The first improvement concerns the polynomial basis in which the Vandermonde matrix is set up (\cref{sec:basis}). We show that the metric shape functions do not depend on this basis, so that its choice is a purely numerical one. To this end, we propose the Lagrange polynomials through the GLL points of the reference square, written in skew coordinates that are scaled to the bounding box of the nodes. On parallelograms, the Vandermonde matrix then becomes a permutation matrix for every $p$. On distorted elements, its condition number is determined by the values of the metric shape functions outside the element, namely at the GLL grid of the bounding box. This traces the remaining ill-conditioning back to the extrapolation of the interpolant beyond the element, and it shows that no choice of basis can reduce the Lebesgue constant.

The second improvement is a hybrid metric-parametric trial space (\cref{sec:hybrid}). Exact reproduction requires only that the trial functions represent the complete polynomials of degree $p$ in $(x,y)$, i.e., $(p+1)(p+2)/2$ of the $(p+1)^2$ functions of an element. Therefore, we write only these in the skew coordinates and the remaining functions in the reference coordinates, in which the nodes form the exact GLL grid on every element. The remaining functions are chosen as the products of Legendre polynomials that are orthogonal to the complete polynomials, which makes the choice unique. The hybrid space is complete, independent of the position, orientation and size of the element, and interpolatory at the nodes, and it coincides with the conventional spectral element on every straight-sided quadrilateral. That is to say, the unsymmetric formulation becomes active only on elements with curved edges, as suggested in the companion paper. Moreover, the hybrid interpolant is the GLL interpolant plus a correction that stems only from the polynomials that the conventional element misses on curved elements.

The numerical studies (\cref{sec:numerics}) first quantify the conditioning, the accuracy of the shape functions and the Lebesgue constants versus the degree and the distortion on single elements. Thereafter, they examine patch tests on distorted and folded meshes, the convergence on meshes whose distortion persists under refinement, the eigenvalue spectra, the transient stability and the thick-cylinder benchmark of Rajendran~\cite{Rajendran2010technique}. In the latter, the error of the finite element solution is compared with the interpolation error of its trial functions, which separates the stability of the unsymmetric system from the approximation property of the trial space. In order to make the trade-off explicit, the studies also document where the hybrid space is less accurate than the original metric space, namely for static problems with smooth solutions at moderate degree and distortion. Note that only linear plane elasticity on quadrilaterals with at most quadratic geometry is considered.

The outline of the remainder of the present article is as follows: First, the unsymmetric spectral element, the conditions for exact reproduction and the metric shape functions in skew coordinates are briefly summarized in \cref{sec:formulation}. Thereafter, the basis of the Vandermonde matrix is discussed in \cref{sec:basis}, before the hybrid trial space is introduced in \cref{sec:hybrid}. The implementation is outlined in \cref{sec:implementation}, and the numerical studies are presented in \cref{sec:numerics}. Finally, the results are discussed in \cref{sec:discussion}, and the most important conclusions are summarized in \cref{sec:conclusions}. For the interested reader, the derivations of the statements used in \cref{sec:formulation,sec:basis,sec:hybrid} are collected in \cref{app:derivations}.
