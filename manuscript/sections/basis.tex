\section{Improvement 1: the basis of the Vandermonde matrix}
\label{sec:basis}
%
\subsection{The basis affects only the round-off error}
\label{sec:basis-invariance}
%
The point of departure is the observation that the metric shape functions are defined by the trial space and the nodes alone. If $\matr V$ in \cref{eq:vandermonde} is regular, the metric functions are the unique functions of the trial space that satisfy $M_i^K(\bx_j)=\delta_{ij}$. They do not depend on the basis $\{\phi_c\}$ in which they are computed. If the basis is replaced by $\bm\psi=\matr G\bm\phi$ with a regular matrix $\matr G$, both $\matr V$ and $\matr P$ are multiplied by $\matr G$ from the left, and $\matr G$ cancels in $\matr V^{-1}\matr P$ (\cref{app:derivations}). Therefore, completeness, independence of the element position and orientation, exact reproduction, the existence of the shape functions and the Lebesgue constant \cref{eq:lebesgue} are properties of the trial space and the nodes. That is to say, the basis affects only the floating-point error. For the solution of $\matr V\matr N_{\mathrm M}=\matr P$ by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting, the standard perturbation bound~\cite{Higham2002Accuracy} gives, as a rule of thumb,
%
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\|\hat{\matr N}_{\mathrm M}-\matr N_{\mathrm M}\|}{\|\matr N_{\mathrm M}\|}\approx\cond(\matr V)\,u ,
  \label{eq:fp}
\end{equation}
%
where $\hat{\matr N}_{\mathrm M}$ denotes the computed shape functions, $\cond(\matr V)=\|\matr V\|\,\|\matr V^{-1}\|$ the condition number of $\matr V$ and $u\approx1.1\cdot10^{-16}$ the unit round-off of double precision. The constants of the rigorous bound, which depend on the size of the matrix and on the pivoting, are of moderate size in practice. Thus, the basis has to be chosen such that $\cond(\matr V)$ is small.
%
\subsection{Candidate bases and bounding-box scaling}
\label{sec:basis-candidates}
%
To this end, we consider tensor-product bases $\phi_c=\varphi_a(\tilde\xi)\,\varphi_b(\tilde\eta)$, $0\le a,b\le p$, with four choices of the one-dimensional functions: (i) the monomials $\varphi_a(\tilde\xi)=\tilde\xi^a$ of the original construction, (ii) the Legendre polynomials $\varphi_a=L_a$, (iii) the Chebyshev polynomials $\varphi_a=T_a$ and (iv) the Lagrange polynomials $\varphi_a=\ell_a$ through the $p+1$ GLL points of $[-1,1]$, which are the one-dimensional factors of the reference shape functions $\hat N_i$. The Legendre and Chebyshev polynomials are evaluated by their three-term recurrences and the Lagrange polynomials by the product formula. The last three bases are designed for the interval $[-1,1]$. On distorted elements, however, the skew coordinates of the nodes leave $[-1,1]^2$, and outside this interval the polynomials grow rapidly, e.g., $|T_p(\bar\xi)|=\cosh\bigl(p\operatorname{arcosh}|\bar\xi|\bigr)$ for $|\bar\xi|>1$. Therefore, we map the bounding box of the nodes in the skew coordinates onto $[-1,1]^2$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\xi=\frac{\bar\xi-c_{\xi}}{r_{\xi}}\qquad\text{with}\qquad
  c_{\xi}=\tfrac12\bigl(\max_j\bar\xi_j+\min_j\bar\xi_j\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad
  r_{\xi}=\tfrac12\bigl(\max_j\bar\xi_j-\min_j\bar\xi_j\bigr),
  \label{eq:bbox}
\end{equation}
%
and likewise for $\tilde\eta$. Here, $\bar\xi_j$ is the first skew coordinate of node $j$, and $c_\xi$ and $r_\xi$ are the center and the half-width of the bounding box in this direction. The scaling is a shift and a stretch of the skew coordinates. Thus, it changes neither the trial space $\Qsk$ nor its completeness, and since $c_\xi$ and $r_\xi$ are computed from the skew coordinates of the nodes, the trial functions remain invariant under affine transformations of the element. On a parallelogram, the skew coordinates of the nodes are the GLL points $\bxi_j$, whose bounding box is $\Kh$, and \cref{eq:bbox} reduces to the identity. The Cartesian derivatives follow from $\nabla_{\bx}=\matr J_0^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(r_{\xi}^{-1},r_{\eta}^{-1})\,\nabla_{\bxit}$, where $\bxit=(\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta)$ denotes the scaled skew coordinates.
%
\subsection{Parallelograms}
\label{sec:basis-affine}
%
On a parallelogram, the nodes form the GLL tensor grid in $(\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta)$. Thus, the two-dimensional Vandermonde matrix is, up to the order of its columns, the Kronecker product of the one-dimensional Vandermonde matrix with itself, and its condition number is the square of the one-dimensional one. For monomials on nodes that are symmetric about the origin, the one-dimensional condition number grows at least exponentially with $p$~\cite{Gautschi1988Lower}, so that $\cond(\matr V)$ grows with the square of an exponential. For the Lagrange basis, on the other hand, every basis function is one at exactly one node and zero at all other nodes. Consequently, $\matr V$ is a permutation matrix (up to the rounding of the scaled skew coordinates of the nodes), its condition number is one, and $\matr N_{\mathrm M}=\matr V^{-1}\matr P$ is obtained without any amplification of rounding errors. The metric and the parametric functions then agree to round-off, and $\matr K_K$ is symmetric to round-off, for every $p$.
%
\subsection{Distorted elements}
\label{sec:basis-distorted}
%
On a non-affine element, the condition number of $\matr V$ in the Lagrange basis has a simple interpretation. Let $\bm g_d$, $d=1,\dots,n$, denote the GLL tensor grid placed in the plane of the scaled skew coordinates, i.e., the GLL grid of the bounding box. With the Lagrange basis, $\phi_c(\bm g_d)$ is one for $c=d$ and zero otherwise. If we choose the grid points $\bm g_d$ as query points in \cref{eq:vandermonde}, $\matr P$ becomes the identity matrix, and $\matr N_{\mathrm M}=\matr V^{-1}$. That is to say, the inverse of the Vandermonde matrix consists of the values of the metric shape functions at the GLL grid of the bounding box. Measured in the 1-norm (largest absolute column sum), this gives (\cref{app:derivations})
%
\begin{equation}
  (\matr V^{-1})_{id}=M_i^K(\bm g_d)\qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \cond_1(\matr V)=\max_j\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}(\bxit_j)\;\max_d\lambda_{\mathcal U}(\bm g_d) ,
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
%
where $\bxit_j$ are the scaled skew coordinates of the element nodes, $\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the Lebesgue function of the GLL interpolation in these coordinates and $\lambda_{\mathcal U}$ that of the metric shape functions \cref{eq:lebesgue}. By \cref{eq:bbox}, all nodes lie in $[-1,1]^2$, and the first factor is bounded by $\Lam_{\mathrm{N}}$, which is at most $6.1$ for $p\le16$. The second factor is the Lebesgue function of the metric shape functions at the GLL grid of the bounding box. On a parallelogram, this grid coincides with the nodes, and the factor is one. On a distorted element, however, part of the grid lies \emph{outside} the element, e.g., at the corners of the bounding box. There, the metric shape functions are extrapolated, and $\lambda_{\mathcal U}$ can exceed the Lebesgue constant $\Lam_{\mathcal U}$, which is the maximum \emph{inside} the element, by orders of magnitude (\cref{sec:num-conditioning}). Thus, \cref{eq:kappa} traces the remaining ill-conditioning back to the extrapolation of the metric shape functions from the element to its bounding box. This effect cannot be removed by switching among the bases considered here, which numerically lie within a factor of about 3 of one another. It could only be removed by bases adapted to the node set itself, e.g., discretely orthogonalized bases~\cite{Brubeck2021Vandermonde}, which is out of the scope of this contribution.

\Cref{eq:kappa} also shows the limit of \emph{any} basis. The Lebesgue function and the Lebesgue constant do not depend on the basis (\cref{sec:basis-invariance}). Therefore, a large Lebesgue constant amplifies perturbations of the nodal values (round-off or the nodal error of a finite element solution) by up to $\Lam_{\mathcal U}$, whatever basis is used. Reducing $\Lam_{\mathcal U}$ requires different trial functions, which is the subject of \cref{sec:hybrid}.

Note that the effect of $\cond(\matr V)$ depends on the nodal data. If the nodal values stem from a polynomial of low degree, e.g., in a patch test, the computed interpolant is accurate in every basis, and its error is limited by the round-off of the nodal values amplified by the Lebesgue constant, $u\,\Lam_{\mathcal U}$. Consequently, the reproduction of polynomials, and with it the patch test, is insensitive to $\cond(\matr V)$. For generic nodal data such as unit vectors, eigenvectors or wave fields, on the other hand, the error is of the order of $\cond(\matr V)\,u$. Since the element matrices are built from the individual shape functions $M_i^K$, i.e., from unit nodal data, their round-off error follows \cref{eq:fp}.
